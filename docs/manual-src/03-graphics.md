## 3.1 The teletype: one line at a time

A PDP-11 in 1975 talked to a person through a **teletype** (a keyboard and a printer on one machine, joined to the computer by a serial line: characters go one after another along a single wire pair, each as a short burst of bits). The machine knew nothing about the page. It could send a character, and the printer put it at the right of the last one; a carriage-return character moved the print head back to the left margin, a line-feed character moved the paper up one line, and that was the whole of its picture of the world. Nothing could be erased, because ink was on paper. This is **line-at-a-time output**: the program's only notion of position is "the end of what I have printed".

At the hardware level it was the same shape as every Unibus device of chapter 2: a few registers. The console of 2.11BSD is a KL11/DL11, declared in `pdp/cons.h` as four words, receive status, receive buffer, transmit status and transmit buffer, at address 0177560 (`cnaddr` in `cons.c`), with the transmitter-ready bit in the transmit status word being `DLXCSR_TRDY`, 0200 (checked in the 2.11BSD tree on this machine). Printing a line was a loop that waits for ready and stores a byte:

```
print_line(string):
    for each byte c in string:
        while (peek(XCSR) & TRDY) == 0:     # the printer is slow: ten characters a second or so (unverified)
            wait
        poke(XBUF, c)
    poke(XBUF, CR); poke(XBUF, LF)          # carriage return, line feed
```

and in PDP-11 assembly, with the register addresses of the console (the fourth word is the transmit buffer; the loop is in the style of the `rk` driver of chapter 2, not copied from a driver):

```
        MOV  #msg, R0
1$:     MOVB (R0)+, R1        ; next character; R0 now points at the one after
        BEQ  3$               ; a zero byte ends the string
2$:     TSTB @#177564         ; XCSR: bit 7 is READY
        BPL  2$               ; plus: not ready, look again
        MOVB R1, @#177566     ; XBUF: this sends it
        BR   1$
3$:
```

Everything a program can say to such a device is a stream of characters. That is why the Unix idea of a **tty** (the name is short for teletype: the device file that a program reads and writes characters from and to) is a stream, and why programs written for it, the shell, the editor `ed`, the compiler's messages, think in lines. The cost shows as soon as the printer is replaced by a screen: the stream model has no way to say "put this here".

## 3.2 The glass teletype and its escape codes

A **video terminal** is a keyboard and a screen on the end of the same serial line. At first it simply imitated the teletype (the "glass teletype": characters appear at the bottom and the page scrolls up) and then it began to do what paper could not: move the cursor to any place and change what was there. The program told it how with **escape sequences**: characters the terminal does not display but obeys, introduced by the escape character (code 27, written ESC), so that the stream carries both text and commands.

Two generations of them matter here. The DEC VT52 was one of the early ones, and the repository's own records preserve one of its codes, cursor-to-position, because it is the textbook example of the problem (c64stuff.txt, the author's chat of 2026-06-18): ESC Y, then a row character and a column character, each with 32 added so that the numbers arrive as printable characters. In pseudocode:

```
goto(row, col):
    send(ESC); send('Y'); send(row + 32); send(col + 32)   # the VT52 form; details unverified
send_string("Hello")                                       # the text appears at the cursor
```

A later and enormously influential generation, the DEC VT100 and the ANSI standard that followed it, uses ESC followed by an opening square bracket, then numbers and a letter: ESC, bracket, 12, a semicolon, 40, `H` moves the cursor to row 12, column 40, and ESC, bracket, 31, `m` makes what follows red (unverified in detail; the repository's records name "VT100/ANSI" and the "xterm-256color" subset as what ordinary programs speak, c64stuff.txt line 218 and 4888). The author's message names a "vt55" between the teletype and the Blit. **The repository's texts name the VT52 and the VT100 and nowhere a VT55, so what "VT55" means is the author's naming** (it may be the VT52 or a model of its family; the DEC model of that number was a graphics terminal, which would fit the author's order, but that is general knowledge and *unverified*); this chapter describes the VT52 and the ANSI codes by what the records say and marks the rest unverified, and it says what a terminal of that kind could not do without relying on the VT55.

What the escape-code terminal gave, and what it did not. It gave a **character-cell display** (a screen that is a grid, 80 columns by 24 rows is the famous size, of cells that each hold one character) that a program could address by position. It did not give a program any control over the shape of a character, a drawing, a mix of fonts or a picture, and it put a slow serial line, with a language of escape codes whose details differed from one make to the next, between the program and its screen. Unix answered the variety with a database of terminal descriptions (**termcap**, then **terminfo**: a file that says, for each make of terminal, which bytes mean "move the cursor") and a library (**curses**: a program that works out the fewest bytes to send to turn the screen as it was into the screen as wanted). The authors of *The UNIX-HATERS Handbook* complain about exactly that ("Original Sin", read for the repository in `docs/kernel-pain-points.md`, section 14I: "Unfortunately for us, Unix was designed in the days of teletypes"). Each character cell needed several bytes of command to change and the bandwidth was low, which is the complaint the next stage answers.

## 3.3 The Blit: a bitmap, and the blit

In the early 1980s the Bell Labs terminal called the **Blit** (Rob Pike, Locanthi and Reiser; the repository records their papers as *Blit 1983* and *Tradeoffs 1985* in `docs/kernel-pain-points.md`, section 14I) took the opposite course. Instead of a terminal that obeys commands about a grid of characters, it had a **bitmap display** (a screen in which every dot, every pixel, is one or more bits in memory, so a program draws anything by changing bits) and a Motorola 68000 processor, 8 MHz, to do the drawing. Its screen memory was in the processor's address space like any other memory. A program that ran in the terminal changed the picture by storing into that memory.

The memory a bitmap needs is a picture stored row after row (the figure shows it in 3.5, where the PC meets it again), and the operation that makes a bitmap usable is the **bit-block-transfer**, written **bitblt** and said "bit blit": copy a rectangle of bits from one place to another, combining each source bit with the destination bit by a chosen rule, the **raster operation** (RasterOp: the rule that says, bit by bit, what the result is, from "replace" to "OR" to "exclusive-OR"; a rule of two inputs has sixteen possible forms, and the Blit authors found that four sufficed: store, OR, clear, XOR, with XOR the most used because drawing a shape twice with it erases it again, which is how a cursor moves without a saved copy of what was beneath it). With bitblt a program scrolls a window (copy the rectangle up by one line of text), moves a window (copy it), draws a letter (copy the letter's rectangle from a stored font into the screen) and fills an area. In pseudocode of the Blit's own library style:

```
bitblt(source_bitmap, source_rect, destination_bitmap, destination_point, operation)
bitblt(font, rect_of('A'), screen, (x, y), OR)       # draws an A with its top left at (x, y)
bitblt(screen, window_rect, screen, window_rect + (0, -line_height), STORE)   # scroll one line
```

The Blit's central point was that the terminal was a small computer, not a protocol. The Blit authors recorded the reason in their own words: "To draw graphics in the windows, sending escape sequences is traditional but makes poor use of the processing power of the terminal"; a predecessor that interpreted escape sequences had failed because "too much state information was maintained by the terminal and guessed at by the Unix program". The Blit received downloaded programs over its serial line (RS-232: a cheap choice, and the authors say that "the greatest problem, though, is certainly the low bandwidth"). The repository's chapters give both halves of that lesson, which the pdp64 keeps: the framebuffer is in the address space, and the blit is an assist that can be bypassed (3.10.6).

The Blit was the inspiration of the window systems that followed it: on the author's account, of rio (Plan 9) and of NeWS (Sun's), and the sources confirm the line from the Blit's `mux` through 8½ to rio; what they were and what the pdp64 could carry over from their simplicity is in 3.19.

One more Blit idea belongs here because the pdp64 inherits the question it raises: the Blit drew its cursor itself, in software, in XOR, at each clock interrupt; other machines had **sprites** (small pictures that the display hardware overlays on the screen, at a position the program gives, without touching the screen memory beneath). The next machine had eight of them.

## 3.4 The Commodore 64: text, graphics, sprites and sound together

The Commodore 64 (1982) was a home computer built around a 6510 processor (a 6502 with a few input and output lines added; unverified detail) and two custom chips: the **VIC-II** (the video interface chip, the video chip) and the **SID** (sound interface device). It was built to play games, and a game needs a lot at once: a background that scrolls, characters that move over it, bright colours, and music and noise. The design answer was to make the chips read their data by themselves from memory and to give the programmer every control as a memory location to **peek** (read a byte) or **poke** (write a byte): the names are the C64 BASIC commands for a load and a store, which is where the author's phrase "peek poke style" comes from.

![The C64 address space](figures/gfx-c64-map.svg)

**Text mode.** The standard screen is 40 columns by 25 rows, 1000 cells. The picture is *two* arrays in memory: screen memory, 1000 bytes, normally at $0400 (the number of the glyph for each cell), and **colour RAM**, 1000 four-bit entries at $D800 (the colour of each cell, one of 16). The shape of each glyph is not in these arrays; it is in the **character ROM** (a read-only memory of 8 bytes a glyph, one byte a row of eight dots, 256 glyphs: 2 KB for one set), which the video chip reads, and which a program can also replace by pointing the chip at glyphs it has made in RAM. To put an A at the top left, in red, the BASIC programmer writes two pokes, and the video chip, which reads screen memory sixty times a second by itself, shows it (the screen codes, the colour numbers and the addresses are general knowledge of the C64, *unverified* here):

```
poke(1024, 1)          # $0400: screen code 1 is the letter A
poke(55296, 2)         # $D800: colour 2 is red
poke(53280, 0)         # $D020: the border colour, black
poke(53281, 6)         # $D021: the background colour, blue
```

This is exactly the "peek poke" model: the screen is memory, a cell is an address, and the chip does the display. No escape codes, no stream: the program that pokes position 1024 + 40 * row + column has put a character there, at any time, in any order.

**Bitmap mode.** The same chip could instead show a bitmap, 320 by 200 dots, in memory, with colour attributes per 8 by 8 block (unverified detail), and **multicolour** modes that trade width for colour. The point the author makes about the C64 is that these were *modes of one chip*, and that the chip could mix them: text in one band of the screen and graphics in another by changing mode at a chosen line.

**Sprites.** The VIC-II had eight **sprites**: movable objects of 24 by 21 dots, each described by 63 bytes of data in memory, a pointer byte (the last eight bytes of screen memory, $07F8 to $07FF, say which 64-byte block holds each sprite's shape) and a few registers (x position, y position, colour, enable, size, priority; collision registers that say which sprites touched). The chip draws the sprites over the background as the picture is read out; to move a sprite, the program changes two registers, and the picture underneath is never disturbed. Sprites were why games were easy: a ship, a bullet, an enemy are each a sprite and a game's movement is a handful of pokes per frame.

**The raster and the raster interrupt.** The VIC-II draws the screen by reading it in order, row by row, while an electron beam sweeps the tube (the **raster**; the whole sweep is a **frame**, 50 or 60 times a second depending on the television standard). A register holds the number of the line being drawn, and another can be set to a line number so that the chip interrupts the processor when the beam reaches it: the **raster interrupt**. Programs used it to change a register mid-screen, for example the background colour or a sprite position, and so to get more than the chip nominally offered, and to make changes while the beam was not looking (the subject of 3.8).

**Sound.** The SID is a three-voice synthesiser with filters, again with registers that the programmer pokes: a voice has a frequency, a waveform (triangle, sawtooth, pulse, noise), and an attack, decay, sustain and release envelope. A note is a handful of pokes (unverified in detail). A game's sound was a part of the same few chips, controlled in the same way, and that is the C64's real lesson: **one machine, one address space, a few chips whose registers are memory, and a program that sets them directly**.

The C64's limits are the limits of 1982: 16 colours, 40 columns, 8 by 8 cells, 8 sprites, three voices. The author's request is for the same ease with none of those limits (3.10).

## 3.5 The PC years: VGA, SVGA, VESA

The IBM PC took a different road. Its first video adapters were separate cards, with their own memory and their own registers, and the software model was a mixture of memory and input/output ports (the I/O instructions that the PDP-11 and the C64 did not have). The **VGA** (Video Graphics Array, IBM, 1987) became the baseline that every later adapter imitated.

**VGA text mode.** The text mode is the C64's idea, with the attribute folded into the cell: the screen is 80 columns by 25 rows, 2000 cells, in memory at 0xB8000, two bytes a cell: the character code and an **attribute byte** (colours: four bits of foreground, three of background, one bit for blink, or for a bright background; the exact layout is *unverified* general knowledge). The shapes come from a font in the adapter's own memory, which a program can replace. To put a red A at the top left:

```
poke(0xB8000, 'A')          # the character
poke(0xB8001, 0x04)         # attribute: black background, red foreground
```

![A VGA text-mode cell](figures/gfx-vga-cell.svg)

**VGA graphics.** The same card had graphics modes, and here the trouble began. Mode 13h (320 by 200, 256 colours, one byte a pixel, at 0xA0000) was the easy one: the framebuffer is a single array and to plot a pixel you write one byte at y * 320 + x. A byte names one of 256 palette entries, and the palette (a table of 256 colours, which the program can load through I/O ports) is in the card (unverified detail). Modes with 16 colours and higher resolutions were **planar**: the four bits of a pixel's colour were stored in four separate **planes** (four arrays, the same pixel position in each), so that to set one pixel's colour the program had to select planes through registers, and the plane that a write reached depended on register settings. It was fast for some operations and a puzzle for the rest. The adapter's window onto its memory was 64 KB at 0xA0000, so any picture larger than 64 KB needed **bank switching** (the card has more memory than the window shows; a register chooses which 64 KB slice appears in the window, and the program must change the register whenever its drawing crosses a slice boundary).

**SVGA and VESA.** Every manufacturer's Super VGA added modes (800 by 600, 1024 by 768, more colours) and, since there was no standard, each used its own registers to switch to them and to select a bank. **VESA** (a standards body of display makers) wrote the **VBE** (VESA BIOS Extensions: calls into the adapter's ROM that list the modes, set one, and switch banks), and a later version let the card present its whole memory as one **linear framebuffer** at a high address, ending the banks (unverified in detail). The picture is now what the chapter has been approaching: a block of memory, a **width** and **height** in pixels, a number of bits per pixel, and a **stride** (also called pitch: the number of bytes from the start of one row to the start of the next, which can be more than the width times the bytes per pixel, because rows are padded to a convenient boundary).

![A framebuffer with a stride](figures/gfx-framebuffer.svg)

**What the programmer had to know.** The price of all this was paid by the programmer, and it was large. A graphics programmer of the late 1980s and the 1990s carried in the head: the VGA's registers, set through ports, that choose which planes a write reaches, how the bits of a write combine with what is already there, and how the card's address counters step; the planar memory layout, in which one pixel's colour is spread over four arrays; **Mode X** (a name from the PC games community for an unofficial VGA mode, 320 by 240 with square pixels, that used the planar memory to give more than one screen of pixels and faster fills; unverified detail); the bank register of each Super VGA chip, which differed from chip to chip, and the per-chip quirks that the VESA calls papered over only in part; the palette, whose entries could be rewritten to make colours cycle or fade without touching the picture; the **retrace**, since a change made at the wrong moment tore, and the program timed its drawing against the beam; and the **inner loops**, written by hand in assembly, for the fill, the copy and the line, because the processor was only just fast enough and a few cycles a pixel decided whether a game ran. The author's referent is the books of Michael Abrash on PC graphics programming, which he recalls as "800 page michael abrash books" (the author's words and figure; the titles, the dates and the page counts are *unverified*: no copy is on this machine and none of the repository's records names a title). The point is not that these programmers did it wrongly: they did it with great skill, and the depth of the books is the measure of what the hardware pushed onto them. The pdp64's aim (3.9) is the opposite: that the program needs none of it.

**The uncomfortable split.** By then the PC had two worlds. In **text mode** the hardware drew the characters, so text was fast, small (4000 bytes) and easy, and a program could not have anything else on the screen. In **graphics mode** a program could have anything, but it drew every dot: even a letter, then, was a program's job (copy its rectangle from a font, which is the Blit's bitblt, now in software), and the text a user had was suddenly slow and unlovely. To change between the two, a program switched modes, the screen blanked, and the other world had none of the conveniences of the first. The C64 had had a mixture (text and bitmap and sprites in one frame); the PC made the programmer choose. Windowing systems, which want text and graphics together, chose graphics and drew everything.

**Why sprites vanished.** The PC games of the VGA era had no sprites (the VGA had none) and drew every moving object into the framebuffer in software, often with the processor doing the work while a second copy of the screen was being prepared (3.8). Three things made this acceptable: the processor became fast enough, by the early 1990s, to move the dots of a screenful in a frame time; the framebuffer was ordinary memory, so any program could write it; and windowing systems composed overlapping windows in software, which made a hardware plane or sprite a special case that the window system would not use. The loss was not free: moving a mouse cursor, a game's ship or a window now cost writing and restoring pixels in the framebuffer, and (3.8) doing it while the display was reading the same pixels.

## 3.6 The GPU: pipelines, vertices, shaders

In the 1990s a new kind of chip took the drawing from the processor: the **GPU** (graphics processing unit), first for three-dimensional games, now for every screen. To see why it looks as it does, start from what a three-dimensional scene is: a list of **vertices** (points in space with a position, and perhaps a colour, a direction the surface faces and a place in a picture), joined into **triangles** (three vertices: the one shape that is always flat, so any surface can be built from them), seen by a camera. To draw it, the machine must (a) work out where on the screen each vertex lands, (b) find which pixels each triangle covers (**rasterization**: turning a shape into the set of pixels it covers), and (c) decide each pixel's colour, which is usually where a **texture** (a picture stored in memory, which is looked up at a position given by the vertex, to paint a surface with, say, brick) comes in. The three steps are the **graphics pipeline**.

![The graphics pipeline](figures/gfx-pipeline.svg)

The early GPUs did each step in fixed hardware. The change that made the modern GPU is that two of the steps became **programmable**: the programmer supplies a **shader** (a small program, written in a special language, that the GPU runs; the name is historical, because shaders began as programs that computed the shading of a surface). A **vertex shader** runs once for every vertex and says where it goes; a **pixel shader** (also called a fragment shader) runs once for every pixel a triangle covers and says what colour it is. The rasterizer between them stays fixed, because it is the same arithmetic for every program.

**Why a GPU is fast.** A processor core is built to run one thread of arbitrary code as quickly as possible: it has a deep pipeline, large caches and a lot of logic to guess and reorder. A GPU is built for the case where *the same small program is run on a very large number of independent items*: a million pixels each need the same few dozen instructions. So it has thousands of simple **lanes** (units that each do arithmetic on one item), grouped so that a group of 32 or 64 lanes (the group size varies by maker; unverified) executes one instruction at the same time on different data (**SIMD**: single instruction, multiple data), and it has memory with an enormous **bandwidth** (bytes per second) to feed them. When one group waits for memory the hardware switches to another, so the lanes are always busy. Three more things make it fast in practice. The CPU does not touch pixels: it describes the work (a **command buffer**: a list of commands in memory, such as "bind this texture, use these shaders, draw these 3,000 vertices as triangles", which the GPU reads and carries out by itself, in the manner of the descriptor rings of chapter 2) and goes on. Work is **batched**: one command can draw thousands of things, so the cost of the command is paid once. And the GPU's memory is organised for it: textures are stored in a layout that keeps neighbouring pixels near each other, and reading a texture is hardware that does the filtering.

The cost is that the GPU is poor at anything with decisions, or with items that depend on each other, and that the programmer must learn a different way of working: describe the whole job as a pipeline, write the shaders, and feed it in large batches. Sections 3.8 and 3.9 show how much of the boilerplate of graphics programming comes from that model.

## 3.7 Why a terminal emulator uses the GPU, and why a font needs three dimensions

A **terminal emulator** (a program that shows a grid of text in a window of a graphical desktop, and acts like the VT-series terminal of 3.2) has a simple job: the screen is a grid of cells, each a glyph with a foreground and a background colour. Several well-known ones draw their grid on the GPU (Alacritty, kitty and WezTerm are the standard examples, and the repository's chat names kitty; the claim that they use it is general knowledge, *unverified* here). Why would a program that shows only text want the machinery built for three-dimensional games?

**The way a GPU draws text.** The text is the same glyphs, over and over. So the program draws each glyph once, with a font rasteriser, into a **glyph atlas** (one texture that holds every glyph the screen uses, side by side), then describes the screen as a list with one entry per cell: where it is, which part of the atlas to show, the two colours. The GPU draws each cell as a **quad** (a rectangle of two triangles) with the glyph's part of the atlas as its texture, and the pixel shader mixes foreground and background by the glyph's value at each pixel. The whole screen, however many cells, is **one draw call** (one command that draws a whole list).

![A glyph atlas and a screen grid of quads](figures/gfx-atlas.svg)

What this buys. The processor never touches a pixel: it changes a cell in the list when the text changes, and scrolling by a line is a change of one number (where the list starts) or a shift of the list, not a copy of two million pixels. Colour and attribute changes cost nothing more. The glyph is stored as coverage values, so the shader can place it at a fraction of a pixel and blend it, which is **antialiasing** (smoothing an edge by giving the pixels it crosses an in-between colour) and **subpixel positioning** (placing a glyph at a position finer than the pixel grid). And the GPU does all of this with the bandwidth of its own memory, leaving the processor free to run the program whose output is being shown.

**Why a font is a three-dimensional problem.** The question deserves an honest answer in two parts. First, a glyph is not a bitmap: in a TrueType font it is an **outline** (curves, in a coordinate system independent of size), and to show it the outline must be turned into pixels, which is **rasterization** again: the same job the GPU's rasterizer does for triangles. A font rasteriser on the processor produces the atlas once. There are also ways of using the GPU for the curves themselves: tessellating the outline into triangles, or evaluating in the pixel shader whether each pixel is inside the curve, or storing a **signed distance field** (a texture in which each pixel holds its distance to the nearest edge of the glyph, positive outside and negative inside, so that a pixel shader can draw the glyph sharply at any size from a small texture; this technique is credited to Valve's 2007 paper, *unverified*). Each of these is the 3D pipeline used on a flat shape.

Second, the pipeline is three-dimensional only by convention. A modern GPU has **no 2D mode**: the 2D accelerator of the 1990s, with its blits, line draws and rectangle fills, has been dropped from the hardware, and the drivers implement a rectangle as two triangles with a shader. Drawing a flat picture is the special case of the 3D machinery in which every z value is the same and the camera looks straight on. This is a choice of the industry and not a necessity: a flat-rectangle engine would be smaller and would do the terminal's job; the reason it is not built is that the GPU has to be built for the harder case anyway, and the 3D case pays for the chip. That is why the *terminal* ends up on the *3D* chip, and it is the reason the pdp64, which will not necessarily have a 3D chip, must decide what to put in its place (3.21).

## 3.8 Scanout, tearing and double buffering

**Scanout.** A display does not hold a picture; it is **refreshed**. The adapter reads the framebuffer from the first row to the last, in order, at a fixed rate, and sends each pixel to the monitor as it is read: this is **scanout** (reading the picture out of memory to the screen). The rate is the **refresh rate**, 60 times a second on most monitors (an example; the C64's was 50 or 60 by television standard). Between one frame and the next, and between rows, there are short **blanking intervals** (times when the monitor is not being sent pixels: the beam on a tube had to fly back, and the name **vertical blank** stuck to the gap between the last row of a frame and the first of the next; VGA called the event the vertical **retrace**). The C64's raster register and raster interrupt of 3.4 are the program's view of the same thing: the number of the row being read now, and a way to be told when it reaches a chosen row.

**Tearing.** Suppose the program changes the picture while the scanout is reading it. The part of the screen above the row being read shows the old picture and the part below shows the new one, and the seam is a **tear**: a horizontal line where an object that moved appears cut in two. A related fault is **flicker**: a program that clears the screen and then draws the new picture lets the scanout catch the screen half-drawn, or empty. The figure shows time to the right and the row being read, rising from the first row to the last once a frame.

![Scanout and tearing against time](figures/gfx-tearing.svg)

**Double buffering.** The standard fix is two pictures. One, the **front buffer**, is being shown; the program draws the next frame in the other, the **back buffer**, and when it is complete the two exchange roles at the moment when the scanout is in the vertical blank, when no row is being read. This is **double buffering**, and the exchange is a **swap** or a **page flip**. The swap can be done by *changing the base address* the scanout reads from (a register write; nothing is copied) or by *copying* the back buffer into the front buffer (a **blit**, cheap on a machine with a blitter and slow on one without, and the copy itself can tear if it is not finished within the blank). The two ways in pseudocode, as a program would write them, in peek/poke style, with the single-buffer way first:

```
# 1. single buffer: tears and flickers
loop:
    clear(FRAME)                          # the scanout can see an empty screen here
    draw_scene(FRAME)                     # and half a scene here
```

```
# 2. double buffer, swap by changing the base address (a page flip), done by polling
front = BUF_A; back = BUF_B
poke(SCANOUT_BASE, front)
loop:
    draw_scene(back)                      # nobody is reading `back`
    while peek(RASTER_LINE) != 0:         # wait for the start of the blank: busy-waiting
        wait                              # (or wait for a vertical-blank interrupt)
    poke(SCANOUT_BASE, back)              # the flip: takes effect now
    swap(front, back)                     # draw next into the picture just retired
```

The cost of double buffering is real. It **needs twice the memory** for the picture: at 1920 by 1080 and 4 bytes a pixel that is 8.3 MB twice (3.15.2). It **needs a deadline**: if drawing the next frame takes longer than a frame time the flip misses the blank, and the program either waits for the next one (the frame rate halves from 60 to 30 and then to 20: the "half frame rate" cost recorded in `kernel-pain-points.md`, 0.6 #3) or flips at once and tears. **Triple buffering** (three pictures, so that the program can start the next frame while the finished one waits for the blank) removes the wait and adds a frame of memory and of **latency** (the delay between the program's input and what the screen shows). Polling the blank occupies the processor; the interrupt version needs a driver. And, where the swap is a base-address register, the register must change *in the blank*, which a program that is itself being interrupted cannot always manage: the Linux display driver for one common graphics chip disables interrupts and busy-waits for the row to pass before it updates registers (the "vblank evasion" of `kernel-pain-points.md` 0.6 #13, from the driver source `intel_pipe_update_start`).

**Is this still a problem?** The author asked (2026-11-10): "with modern hdmi is tearing even a concern?" The honest answer is that tearing is not a property of HDMI at all. HDMI (like DVI and DisplayPort, 3.14) carries a stream of pixels at a steady rate, one row after another, and has no idea whether the picture it is carrying was complete when it was read. A tear happens one step earlier, in the display controller that reads the framebuffer out while a program is changing it; the cable only repeats what it was given. On a phone or a desktop it is largely hidden, because between the program and the controller there is a **compositor** (a program of the system that collects the pictures of all the windows and builds the one that is shown) and a driver, and together they draw the next frame in a spare buffer and flip it at the vertical blank: tear-free by construction. That machinery *is* the boilerplate of 3.9, which the author wants the architecture to sugar away. A program on the pdp64 that writes straight into the framebuffer, with no operating system in between (as a boot program, a game written to the metal or a C64-style program would), can still tear, so a tear-free flip has to come from the architecture or the device, not from the cable. Variable refresh (HDMI VRR, adaptive sync) turns a late frame into a short delay in the display instead of a tear or a missed blank, which reduces the need for a blank-time flip but does not remove it: the frame still must not change *while* it is read. Other displays add their own behaviour: a television may hold a whole frame before showing it (added **latency**), and a laptop panel may refresh itself from its own memory when nothing changes (panel self-refresh). Of this, what is *checked* is only what the repository and the headers say: that HDMI is a pixel-stream transmitter (3.14.1) and that the DRM interface on this machine has a page-flip call with an event on completion and an asynchronous flag that allows a flip with tearing (`DRM_MODE_PAGE_FLIP_EVENT` and `DRM_MODE_PAGE_FLIP_ASYNC` in `drm_mode.h`). What is *general knowledge, unverified*: the HDMI VRR and adaptive-sync standards and what a sink does with a late frame, the latency of a television's processing, panel self-refresh, and what phone and desktop compositors do. Whether a tear-free flip is worth hardware if the display side is variable-refresh is a question for the author (3.23).

**What the repository already decides.** The video device of 3.10 (`VIC-64X`) already has the first piece of sugar: every register that takes effect at a frame boundary is shadowed, one write to its `ARM` register latches them all together at the start of the next vertical blank, a status bit says whether the arm was taken in this frame or missed it, a sticky underrun bit reports a scan-out that ran dry, and the vertical-blank interrupt occurs at a fixed line (statement 3.10.9, errata 71, from the pain-point list of `kernel-pain-points.md`). A base address in that register set therefore flips at the blank without any program arranging it. The chat of the author's device design had said the same thing as a rule in 2026-08 ("multi-register updates are atomic at a named raster line or vblank", `docs/c64-devices-from-chat.md` section 2) and, for the eight-way machine, "flip on vsync unconditionally with an overrun interrupt instead of a barrier". What no text yet decides is anything beyond one shadowed register set: independent layers, damage, variable rate, fences, and a default that a program does not have to set up.

**3.8.1** The architecture is to offer conveniences that avoid tearing, flicker and the other faults that forced programmers to double-buffer, and to do it so that the common program needs no more than a store; the form of those conveniences is open. *(P)* (The author, 2026-11-10: "we want to put in nice architectural sugar to avoid 'tearing' and all those other issues graphics programmers had to use double buffering to fix", errata 105.)

**3.8.2** The mechanism that is decided is the shadowed registers and the one `ARM` write of 3.10.9; everything else in this section is a candidate. *(D)*

The candidates, each with what it costs, and none decided:

* **A scanout base register that latches at the vertical blank** (a page flip: the register holds the address of the picture to show and takes the new value only in the blank), with a completion **interrupt** or a **fence** (a counter that the device advances when the flip has happened, which the program can read or wait on, so it knows that the old picture is free). *Gives:* no tearing, no polling of the raster; the program writes one register and goes on. *Costs:* two pictures of memory for the program that uses it, and a rule for what the register shows if it is written twice in one frame (the first is dropped, or the second waits). *Already in 3.10.9, except the fence.*
* **Per-layer latching**, in a design with layers (3.21, sketches 1 and 2): each layer has its own base register and flips independently, so a cursor or a sprite layer can move every frame while the text layer flips only when its text changes. *Gives:* the cheapest case for text, a moving cursor with no redraw. *Costs:* one set of registers per layer.
* **An atomic commit of several registers**: several registers are staged and applied together at the blank (this is what `ARM` of 3.10.9 is for the single set; Linux calls the same idea "atomic modesetting" and offers `DRM_MODE_ATOMIC_TEST_ONLY` to ask "would this work?" and `DRM_MODE_ATOMIC_NONBLOCK` to ask for it without waiting, in `drm.h` and `drm_mode.h` on this machine). *Gives:* a change of mode, picture and cursor that cannot be seen half-done. *Costs:* the shadow copies of the registers, which a small device pays in flip-flops; an optional test-before-commit is more.
* **A raster-position register and a raster interrupt** (the C64 way): the program can know the row being read and be interrupted at a chosen row. *Gives:* mid-frame changes (the C64's trick of a different colour or sprite position per band) and a way to time drawing behind the beam. *Costs:* one register and an interrupt source; encourages programs that are tied to the display's timing.
* **Variable refresh** (the monitor waits for the frame instead of reading at a fixed rate: so a frame that is late is not a missed blank but a short wait; the standards are called adaptive sync, FreeSync, G-Sync and, for HDMI, VRR, variable refresh rate; unverified general knowledge). *Gives:* a late frame costs only its own delay, not a halving of the rate. *Costs:* the output must support it (the HDMI transmitter chip of 3.14.1 and the monitor), and the device's mode registers must say so.
* **Damage rectangles** (the program says which part of the picture changed and only that part is sent on): this is what virtio-gpu's `TRANSFER_TO_HOST_2D` and `RESOURCE_FLUSH` take as a `virtio_gpu_rect` (3.16). *Gives:* less memory traffic when little changes (a terminal). *Costs:* the program must track what it changed.
* **A hardware cursor and sprites**: objects that the scanout overlays at a position without touching the picture under them, so moving one never needs a redraw and never tears. *Gives:* the cursor, and in a game its moving objects, for the price of two registers each. *Costs:* gates in the scanout path and a limit on how many. (virtio-gpu has a cursor in its protocol: a separate cursor queue, `UPDATE_CURSOR` and `MOVE_CURSOR`, `virtio_gpu.h`.)
* **Versioned or copy-on-write buffers**: the device keeps the picture the scanout is reading and the program writes to a fresh version that replaces it atomically when the program says it is done, so the program can never see or show a half-drawn frame and never has to name two buffers. *Gives:* the sugar nearest to "nothing to do": a program draws and says `commit`. *Costs:* the memory and copying are the device's; a page-granular copy-on-write needs the memory-management machinery of chapter 6 or a device-side equivalent; a whole-frame version is twice the memory with a rule of who allocates it.
* **A fence or sequence counter**: a register that the device increments when each commit has been applied, which the program can read (poll) or wait for on an interrupt, to know when a buffer is free. *Gives:* synchronisation with no guess. *Costs:* a counter and a rule for wraparound.

And the same loop, in the style the pdp64 could offer, written with three of the candidates (a latching base register, a fence, and the interrupt) in the form that the program meets:

```
# 3. the flip at the blank, with the sugar: no polling of the raster, no waiting for the flip to be safe
poke(SCANOUT_BASE, front)
loop:
    draw_scene(back)
    poke(SCANOUT_BASE, back); poke(ARM, 1)    # staged now, latched by the device at the next blank (3.10.9)
    wait_until(peek(FLIP_COUNT) >= expected)  # a fence: the old picture is free (a candidate, not defined)
    swap(front, back)
```

Which sketch gives which sugar cheaply (3.21): the layer sketches (1 and 2) give per-layer latching and a hardware cursor and sprites almost for free, because the compositing is in the scanout and the shadowing is in its registers; the command-queue sketch (3) gives the fence for free (a command-queue device already completes commands in order and signals them) and the atomic commit (a queue entry is applied whole); the virtio-gpu route (4) gives damage rectangles and fences (both are in its protocol) and nothing about the raster. The names `FLIP_COUNT` and `ARM` above are placeholders; the repository defines an `ARM` register only as a name in 3.10.9.

## 3.9 What made graphics programming annoying

The author, 2026-11-10: "basically, anything that made graphics programming annoying or cumbersome or boiler platey, we want to sugar away to almost nothing." That is a goal and not a design: it says what the architecture should aim at, and the means, device by device, are open. **Boilerplate** (code that has to be written the same way in every program before the program can do anything of its own, which carries no idea of the program's) is what the goal is about, so the way to take it seriously is to list the boilerplate that exists, show it, and for each piece ask what the architecture could do so that the program does not have to.

**3.9.1** The aim is that what made graphics programming annoying, cumbersome or boilerplate is reduced by the architecture to almost nothing; the means are open and each is a candidate (section 3.9 and its table). *(P)* (The author, 2026-11-10, errata 105.)

**3.9.2** The standard first-iteration device, virtio-gpu, keeps its own protocol (3.16); where this section says that a sugar "would" appear, it appears in a device of the second iteration (3.10) or as a library over virtio-gpu, and no sugar is specified. *(S)*

**3.9.3** The measure that every design in this chapter is held to is the author's: "graphics programming should be as simple and pleasurable as the c64 was." *(P)* (2026-11-10, errata 105; a goal, not a design.)

Before the pairs, the measure of the burden. The books of Michael Abrash on VGA and PC graphics programming (the author's "800 page michael abrash books"; titles and page counts unverified, see 3.5) exist because a programmer could not get a fast picture without knowing the VGA's registers, its planar memory, its retrace, the banking of each chip and how to hand-tune the inner loops; a book of that size is what it took to teach it. Each row of the table below is a piece of that knowledge, or its modern successor (the long lists of the Linux display interface and of Vulkan), that the author would like the architecture to make unnecessary. It is said with respect for the craft: the knowledge was real, and it was needed.

Three worked pairs. Each shows the boilerplate as the real systems have it (the names are those of the real interfaces, checked in the headers on this machine where marked, otherwise general knowledge, *unverified*), and then what the pdp64 would offer. The right-hand versions are sketches and use placeholder names; they are not registers.

**Pair 1: getting a picture on the screen at all.** On a VGA/VESA PC the program asked the BIOS for the list of modes, picked one, set it, and found the framebuffer:

```
# VESA (VBE), 1990s: about 15 lines in a real program (illustrative, not measured)
info = vbe_get_controller_info()
for m in info.mode_list:
    mi = vbe_get_mode_info(m)
    if mi.width == 1024 and mi.height == 768 and mi.bits_per_pixel == 32 and mi.has_linear_framebuffer:
        mode = m
vbe_set_mode(mode | LINEAR_FRAMEBUFFER_BIT)
fb = map_physical(mi.physical_base, mi.pitch * mi.height)
```

On Linux today (DRM/KMS: the kernel's display interface) the same job is longer. The program opens the device, lists **connectors** (the outputs), reads the monitor's list of modes (**EDID**: the data block that the monitor supplies describing the modes it supports, a general-knowledge name), chooses one (`struct drm_mode_modeinfo`: pixel clock, horizontal and vertical total and sync positions, refresh rate, in `drm_mode.h`), allocates a **dumb buffer** (a plain pixel buffer), registers it as a framebuffer (`DRM_IOCTL_MODE_ADDFB2`, with a format code and a pitch), maps it, and sets the mode on a **CRTC** (the unit that does scanout; the name is from cathode-ray tube controller):

```
# Linux DRM/KMS, one output, no atomic: about 40 lines in a real program (illustrative, not measured)
fd = open("/dev/dri/card0")
res = drmModeGetResources(fd)
conn = first connected connector in res
mode = conn.modes[0]                           # the monitor's preferred mode, by convention first
crtc = a crtc the encoder of conn can use
buf = create_dumb(fd, mode.hdisplay, mode.vdisplay, 32)    # returns handle, pitch, size
fb_id = drmModeAddFB2(fd, w, h, XRGB8888, handle, pitch)
ptr = mmap(fd, map_dumb(buf).offset, size)
drmModeSetCrtc(fd, crtc, fb_id, 0, 0, conn, mode)
```

A program on Vulkan or OpenGL does not do that itself but meets another list: create an instance, enumerate physical devices, choose a queue family, create a logical device, create a window surface, create a swap chain and its images, create image views, a render pass, framebuffers, command pools and buffers and synchronisation objects (the names `vkCreateInstance`, `vkCreateSwapchainKHR` and the like are Vulkan's; the headers are not on this machine, *unverified*; OpenGL's `glGenBuffers`, `glCompileShader`, `glLinkProgram` are in `glext.h` here). A "hello triangle" in Vulkan is commonly several hundred lines (general knowledge, illustrative). In virtio-gpu the equivalent is the six commands of 3.16, about 25 lines of the real driver: create resource, attach backing, set scanout, draw, transfer, flush.

What the pdp64 could offer is **a device that already shows something**:

```
# the pdp64 way, open (a sketch)
# the device powers up with a sane default mode, in the monitor's preferred mode, with a canonical pixel format:
fb = peek(DISPLAY_BASE)            # where the picture is, always mapped
w  = peek(DISPLAY_WIDTH); h = peek(DISPLAY_HEIGHT)
poke(fb + (y * w + x) * 4, 0xFFFFFFFF)     # a white pixel: done
```

Options for the sugar, with trade-offs. (a) The device **reports its mode and a sane default** (a register holds the width, the height and the refresh rate of the mode chosen at start-up from the monitor's data, as the virtio-gpu `GET_DISPLAY_INFO` already returns the width and height of each output in a `virtio_gpu_resp_display_info`); nothing to set. *Cost:* the device reads the EDID itself (a small I2C engine, which 3.14.1 already puts at the transmitter chip) and picks a policy; a program that wants another mode still has the registers. (b) A **fixed canonical pixel format** with the device converting at scanout (the repository's 48-bit RGBA pixel, errata 73, would be that format on the native device; virtio-gpu offers only 32-bit formats with eight bits per channel: `VIRTIO_GPU_FORMAT_B8G8R8A8_UNORM` and its siblings, in `virtio_gpu.h`, so that the 48-bit pixel does not exist on the standard device, an inconsistency that is listed in 3.23). *Cost:* a conversion stage in the scanout, and memory a program wastes if it wants fewer bits. (c) **Stride equal to width** unless the program asks for padding, and the framebuffer window **always mapped**: the program has no `mmap`, no bank register, no `map_physical`; the window is in its address space from the start, a decision of the page tables and the bus (2.3.3 and 3.15.4). *Cost:* a fixed place in the address space, and a rule for what a program does that wants two framebuffers.

**Pair 2: the cache and the flush.** On a modern computer the processor has a **cache** (a small fast memory that keeps recently used data close to the core). When the program writes pixels they may be in the cache and not yet in memory, and a device that reads memory by DMA sees the old data. Programs on non-coherent systems therefore have to flush:

```
# ARM or x86 with a non-coherent display: about 3 lines per update, in every program (illustrative)
draw(framebuffer)
cache_clean_range(framebuffer, size)       # push the dirty lines to memory
memory_barrier()                           # and make sure the writes are ordered before the notification
poke(DOORBELL, 1)
```

The pdp64 already has this sugar, and chapter 2 says how: processor caches are coherent with each other, a device and the processor are not, and the answer is not a flush instruction but a memory type, **uncached**: "buffers are mapped uncached and the hand-off is explicit" (2.5.2), and "no driver can forget a cache clean or invalidate, because there is none to forget" (2.5 commentary); the framebuffer is mapped uncached too (3.15.1). The cost, which the commentary of 3.15 states, is that a long run of writes is no slower per byte but a single write is slow; the candidate improvement is a write-combining memory type for the framebuffer (3.15, Modern comparables), which the architecture's four memory types of 2.3.4 do not name. The sugar is therefore decided for correctness (D) and open for speed (P).

**Pair 3: drawing text.** On a GL or Vulkan system text is the longest boilerplate of all, because the GPU has no text:

```
# a GPU text path (illustrative; about 150 lines in a real small program, not measured)
font = ft_load("DejaVuSansMono.ttf"); ft_set_size(font, 16)
atlas = new_texture(1024, 1024)
for each character c needed:
    bitmap = ft_render_glyph(font, c)           # the font rasteriser (CPU)
    rect = pack_into_atlas(atlas, bitmap)       # find room: a bin-packing problem
    upload(atlas, rect, bitmap)
    remember(c, rect, advance, bearing)         # metrics: how far to move on, where the glyph sits
for each cell on screen:
    append_quad(vertex_list, x, y, rect_of(char), fg, bg)
upload(vertex_list); bind(atlas, shader); draw(vertex_list)
# plus kerning, ligatures, right-to-left runs, combining marks, fallback fonts, scaling for HiDPI
```

The pdp64's terminal would do it as the C64 did, with one store:

```
# the pdp64 way, open (a sketch; names are placeholders)
poke(CELL(row, col).glyph, SLOT_OF('A'))     # or: poke(CELL(row, col).char, 'A')
poke(CELL(row, col).fg, RED)
# a new glyph: one library call fills a slot from the TrueType engine
font_render_into_slot(font, 'λ', slot)       # rasterises the outline and writes the bitmap into the slot
```

Here the author's glyph slots do the work of the atlas, and a library call replaces the first half of the boilerplate (3.11). The candidates are (a) the cell holds the **character code**, and the device or a table maps the code to a slot, so the program never names slots (the author's c64x terminal of 2026-06-18, `c64stuff.txt`, line 5015: "each cell holds its actual data, not a reference to the data"); (b) the cell holds the **slot number** directly, which is the simplest hardware and makes the program responsible for assigning slots. The trade-off, which is open (3.23): (a) is nicer for the program and costs a lookup table in the scanout; (b) is nearly free and puts a small allocator in a library.

## 3.10 The terminal: VIC-64X, SID-64X and BLT64

**3.10.1** The graphics terminal is based on two machines, not one: the **Commodore 64**, for its video and sound as chips with registers that a program writes, and the **Blit terminal** (Rob Pike's bit-mapped windowing terminal), for its framebuffer in the address space and the bit-block-transfer operation that moves rectangles of pixels. The pdp64 modernises and extends both. *(D)*

**3.10.2** The video device `VIC-64X` is the Commodore 64's video chip (the VIC-II) extended: a framebuffer in the address space with 48-bit pixels, scanned out through a line buffer. A pixel is RGBA with 12 bits per channel (errata 73, the author's decision of 2026-10-24). The register formats and the other pixel formats are unspecified (3.13). *(D)*

**3.10.3** The sound device `SID-64X` is the C64's sound chip (the SID) extended: voices with filters, and also a general sound path that is not limited to the SID's voices; audio samples are 48 bits (errata 74, the author's decision of 2026-10-24). The register formats and the layout of a 48-bit sample are unspecified (3.13). *(D)*

**3.10.4** The blitter `BLT64`, the Blit's bit-block-transfer operation as a device, copies and combines rectangles of pixels with raster operations: registers for the source and destination base addresses, the strides, the width and height, a raster operation (the sixteen binary operations of source against destination and the common ternary ones), a mask or transparent colour, and a control register with direction, go, busy and interrupt on done. The device chat chose a three-operand `draw` instead (`decisions-pending.md` E3). *(P)*

**3.10.5** Each of the three is a device in a slot: it is enumerated by the bus, mapped into its own 4 KB page, and found by its identification register like any other (chapters 2 and 5). The device chat put the C64 devices in one 8 KB window per process (`decisions-pending.md` E5). *(P)*

**3.10.6** The framebuffer stays plain memory, so that software can draw without `BLT64`; the blitter is an assist that can be bypassed, not the only way to draw (the Blit authors' advice in 1985 was "transparent, so it can be bypassed"). *(P)*

**3.10.7** Four raster operations are required of `BLT64`: store, OR, clear and exclusive-OR (these four sufficed on the Blit, and exclusive-OR dominated its use); the other twelve binary operations and the ternary ones are optional. *(P)*

**3.10.8** Whether the video device has a hardware cursor or sprite is unspecified (the vertical-blank interrupt is 3.10.9); the Blit read the mouse position at the retrace interrupt and drew its cursor in software, in exclusive-OR. *(S)*

**3.10.9** Every register of the video device that takes effect at a frame boundary is shadowed: one write to its `ARM` register latches them all together at the start of the next vertical blank, and a status bit says whether the arm was taken in this frame or missed it. A sticky underrun bit and an underrun counter report a scan-out that ran dry. The vertical-blank interrupt occurs at a fixed line of the frame, whose number a register holds. The sound path's audio clock relation (3.14.4) is part of the same mode setting. *(D)*

*Commentary.* **Precedent.** The Commodore 64's VIC-II and SID were chips whose registers a program poked directly, with a screen in memory that the video chip read by direct memory access; **the Blit**, Rob Pike's terminal, had a framebuffer in the address space and a bit-block-transfer operation that the terminal did in software (Pike, Locanthi and Reiser, 1985: the terminal had "no pixel-moving hardware"), where the Amiga and other 1980s machines added hardware blitters. The Blit was a Motorola 68000 machine (8 MHz) that received downloaded code, characters and editing messages from its host over a serial line and drew every pixel itself; its description as a precursor of the X11 protocol and of the NeWS window system is not stated by any source read *(S)*, while the lineage the sources confirm runs from the Blit's `mux` to 8½ and `rio` (`docs/kernel-pain-points.md` section 14I). The display was a small computer driven by messages, where here it is a set of devices on the bus driven by registers and descriptors, so the pdp64 needs no display protocol between host and terminal (the nearest thing is the descriptor ring of chapter 5); the pdp64's terminal takes the framebuffer and the bit-block-transfer operation from the Blit (the pdp64 adds the hardware engine, `BLT64`) and the video and sound chips from the C64, and both are modernised. The Blit authors give the cost of the split (a slow link, and a program in two halves) as the reason their design was what it was; the pdp64 avoids the split by putting the display on the bus. Plan 9 later moved its window system into files (`/dev/bitblt`, then `/dev/draw`) for the sake of size and remote use: a stream of small commands written to a device, the same shape as an entry in a descriptor ring (reading of the commands as a ring is an inference, not a source's statement). **Trade-offs.** The blitter covers scrolling a terminal, moving a window, drawing a glyph, filling a rectangle and exclusive-oring a cursor, which is the working set of a text-and-windows machine; it does not do three-dimensional graphics. `MCPY` is no substitute: it is one-dimensional and occupies the processor, where a blit has a stride and a raster operation and runs concurrently. 48-bit pixels (presumably 16 bits per channel) exceed what any display connector carries today, so the display device converts to the connector's depth on the way out; 48-bit audio samples likewise exceed what a converter takes, so the sound device keeps the precision for mixing and filtering and reduces it at the output. **Improvements.** The C64's video and sound registers were scattered and irregular; the pdp64 versions use the uniform register face, so one driver skeleton serves them. **Benefits.** The blitter is about 1,500 lookup tables and a line buffer, nearly all address arithmetic; the whole terminal is a handful of small devices. **Modern comparables.** Every embedded display controller has a 2-D blitter and a scan-out engine; sound chips are audio codecs behind an I2S interface. **Impact.** *Kernel:* a framebuffer, a sound and a blit driver are each a mapped page and a few register writes; no cache maintenance is needed because the framebuffer is uncached. *Compiler:* none. *Hardware:* a scan-out engine, a line buffer, a small address-arithmetic unit for the blitter and a mixer for the sound device, each independent of the processor and of the others.

## 3.11 The terminal as the author describes it: peek/poke text and graphics, and glyph slots

The author, 2026-11-10: "our virtio-console device is an attempt to bring the nice properties of the blit terminal and c64 for doing simple and easy peek poke style display of text and also doing graphics, but with modern color depths and screen sizes, and with glyph maps we can even have a ttf engine generate glyphs and they will go into their memory slots for use."

**3.11.1** The pdp64's terminal is meant to bring the properties of the Blit terminal and the C64 that the earlier sections describe: a display that a program drives by simple peeks and pokes, for text and for graphics together, at modern colour depths and screen sizes; and a **glyph map** (a table of slots, each a place in memory for the picture of one glyph) that a TrueType engine fills, so that the shapes of the characters are made by software and used by the display through their slots. *(P)*

**3.11.2** What the author calls "our virtio-console device" is not specified in this repository: no document defines a device of that name, its identification, its queues or its registers, and the form of the terminal device (a new virtio device, an extension of virtio-gpu, or the native `VIC-64X` family of 3.10) is **open**. *(S)*

*The difference, stated exactly, and not resolved.* The repository's chapter 3 describes the native devices `VIC-64X`, `SID-64X` and `BLT64`, and these were **deferred** by the author's decision of 2026-11-03 (errata 78) and made obsolete as a model by "forget the native 8 register devices. gone. virtio across the board." (errata 95, 2026-11-09). The standard video and audio devices are **virtio-gpu in 2D mode and virtio-snd** (errata 79). **virtio-console**, in the virtio specification as the Linux header `virtio_console.h` on this machine shows it, is a *serial text device*: device type 3 (`VIRTIO_ID_CONSOLE`), a configuration structure of `cols`, `rows`, `max_nr_ports` and `emerg_wr`, the feature bits `SIZE`, `MULTIPORT` and `EMERG_WRITE`, and control messages for ports (`PORT_ADD`, `PORT_REMOVE`, `RESIZE`, ...); the data are byte streams over queues, one receive and one transmit queue for each port; it carries no cells, colours, glyphs or pixels. In this repository virtio-console appears only as a candidate for the *console* of the boot ROM and the kernel (statement 5.5.5 and the open question Q6 of the decision records: "virtio-console optional for ordinary use", beside "a minimal fixed UART kept for the boot ROM and panic"), that is as a text stream like the teletype of 3.1, not as a display. So "virtio-console" as the author uses it names a device with properties (cells, colours, glyph slots, graphics) that **neither** the virtio-console of the specification **nor** virtio-gpu 2D has, and that the native devices were to have. Four readings are possible and this chapter chooses none: (1) a new virtio device type of the pdp64's own, with the cell array and glyph slots as the device's memory; (2) virtio-gpu extended by a text layer and glyph slots; (3) virtio-console used as the text *stream* (the path for existing tty programs, 3.2) beside a separate display device; (4) the native device family renamed. The choice is the first question of 3.23.

**What the author's earlier terminal chat adds** (2026-06-18, recorded in `docs/c64-devices-from-chat.md` section 6; a separate project, not a pdp64 decision): a cell holds a 32-bit foreground colour, a 32-bit background colour, a UTF-8 character and about eight bits of attributes (bold, italic, underline, blink); "the number of cells take up so little memory, the 64 bits devoted to color is trivial"; "each cell holds its actual data, not a reference to the data"; sprites are a separate plane that composites over the cells; existing tty programs must run through a shim and native programs use the terminal fully, "peek and poke is the internal interface". That chat-era design fits the cell array of the figure below; it differs from the author's new words in one place: **the cell there holds the character code itself, and the glyph map of the new words implies a slot** (3.9, pair 3, candidate (a) against (b)).

![A cell array and a glyph slot table](figures/gfx-cells-glyphs.svg)

**3.11.3** A glyph map is a table of **slots**; a slot holds the picture of one glyph (for example, a rectangle of coverage values, one byte for each pixel, which tells how much of the pixel the glyph covers, so that the display can antialias), and the screen is an array of cells each of which names a glyph (by slot, or by a character code that is looked up to a slot) and gives its colours and attributes. A TrueType engine, which is software, rasterises a glyph from the font's outline at the pixel size of the display and writes the result into a slot; a glyph is made once and used by every cell that names it. The layout of the slot (its width, height, pixel format, and whether a slot may hold several cells' width, for a wide glyph) and the number of slots are unspecified. *(S)*

As an arithmetic example, with the chat-era cell (about 13 bytes: 4 + 4 + 4 for a character + 1 for attributes) and a 1920 by 1080 screen of 8 by 16 pixel cells, there are 240 columns and 67 rows, 16,080 cells and about 209 KB of cells; with slots of 16 by 32 coverage bytes (512 bytes) and 4,096 slots, the glyph map is 2 MB. Both are small against a 12.4 MB frame at 48 bits (3.15.2), which is the author's observation of 2026-06-18: the cells are small, and text is cheap to store. These are examples, not decisions.

**3.11.4** Whether the terminal has sprites is not stated in the author's description of 2026-11-10; the C64 had them and the earlier terminal chat gave sprites a separate plane, and statement 3.10.8 leaves the pdp64's hardware cursor and sprites unspecified. *(S)*

## 3.12 A peek/poke program for the terminal

This is what the author's description would let a program do. **No register of the terminal is specified** (3.13), so the names are placeholders in capitals, to be replaced by the real ones when the device is defined; where a number appears it is an example.

```
# placeholders: SCREEN is the base of the cell array, COLS its width in cells,
#               GLYPHS the base of the glyph map, GLYPH_SIZE the size of one slot

put_char(row, col, ch, fg, bg):                    # one cell: a few stores
    cell = SCREEN + (row * COLS + col) * CELL_SIZE
    poke32(cell + GLYPH, slot_of(ch))              # which glyph (or the character code, 3.11)
    poke32(cell + FG, fg)                          # colours, modern depth: the pixel format of 3.10.2
    poke32(cell + BG, bg)
    poke8 (cell + ATTR, 0)

define_glyph(slot, font, codepoint):               # a glyph the program has just thought of
    bitmap = ttf_rasterise(font, codepoint, CELL_W, CELL_H)   # software: the TrueType engine
    copy(GLYPHS + slot * GLYPH_SIZE, bitmap, GLYPH_SIZE)      # an ordinary memory copy

put_char(0, 0, 'A', RED, BLACK)                    # an A, red on black, top left: four stores
define_glyph(200, my_font, 0x03BB)                 # a lambda goes into slot 200
put_char(0, 1, 200, WHITE, BLACK)                  # and is used by naming slot 200
```

Graphics beside text, in the C64 manner, are another array and one more register set:

```
# the graphics plane: a framebuffer of FB_W by FB_H pixels (3.10.2: 48 bits, or 32 on the standard device)
put_pixel(x, y, colour):
    poke32(FB_BASE + (y * FB_STRIDE + x) * 4, colour)       # stride from the register FB_STRIDE
# sprites, if the design has them (3.11.4: not decided):
poke(SPRITE_N + X, 100); poke(SPRITE_N + Y, 80); poke(SPRITE_N + SHAPE, shape_slot)
```

Three observations. Each line is one store, so the program is as small as the C64's was. Everything is memory, so `memcpy` fills a glyph slot as it fills any buffer. And the *anti-annoyance* of 3.9 is visible in what is missing: no mode setting, no format conversion, no flush (the memory type of 3.15.1 does that), no atlas to pack, no shader, no command buffer. What the terminal is *not* is a three-dimensional machine; for that the standard device, virtio-gpu, remains (3.16, 3.17).

**3.13.2** What is fixed: the devices exist, they follow the eight-register convention in the terminal's slots, and the interrupt, `FENCE` and uncached-framebuffer rules of section 3.15 apply. *(S)*

## 3.14 How the terminal drives HDMI and the other ports

**3.14.1** The display connector is standard kit: HDMI (and DVI and DisplayPort) is a transmitter chip that takes pixels and sync signals in parallel and does the link protocol, the encoding and the electrical signalling itself; on the first board it is the DE10-Nano's ADV7513, set up once over an I2C-style control bus. *(D)*

**3.14.2** The video device's output side is therefore a pixel stream with horizontal and vertical sync, a clock and a data-enable, which is what every such transmitter accepts; the device's registers set the mode (width, height, blanking, pixel clock, the pixel format), the base address of the framebuffer and the display on and off. *(P)*

**3.14.3** Other ports are the same shape: a different transmitter (VGA digital-to-analogue converter, DisplayPort, an LVDS panel) takes the same pixel stream, so the pdp64's side does not change, and each port is one more slot or one more output of the video device. *(P)*

**3.14.4** The sound device's output is likewise a standard digital audio stream (I2S or the HDMI audio channel) into a converter or the transmitter. The processor never sees the HDMI packets, but whoever sets the mode must also program the audio clock relation: the transmitter needs N (and CTS, or a hardware CTS calculation) for each pixel clock and sample rate, and a driver such as the ADV7511's computes both. *(P)*

**3.14.5** The ports that carry data, SATA for storage and USB for peripherals, follow the same pattern: the protocol is in the chip or controller block, and the processor's side is a slot with the common registers and a ring (chapter 5). *(D)*

*Commentary.* **Precedent.** The MiSTer framework on the DE10-Nano already does exactly this (the retro cores produce a pixel stream and the framework's scaler and the HDMI transmitter do the rest), which is why the plan buys HDMI there. **Trade-offs.** The control bus of the transmitter needs a small amount of one-time setup that a boot routine does by writing a few registers; the pixel clock must be generated to the mode, which on an FPGA is a phase-locked loop and in silicon is the same block. **Improvements.** Because the interface is a stream and a register set, replacing the transmitter or the connector changes nothing above the video device, as a SATA controller behind its ring can be replaced without changing the disk driver. **Benefits.** No part of the operating system carries an HDMI, SATA or USB protocol stack: each is one driver of the common shape. **Modern comparables.** Single-board ARM computers with a display controller and an HDMI transmitter; the video output of every SoC. **Impact.** *Kernel:* a display driver sets a mode and a base address and nothing else; hot-plug of a monitor is a bus event if the transmitter reports one. *Compiler:* none. *Hardware:* a pixel generator, a clock and an off-chip transmitter; no protocol logic in the pdp64's own gates.

## 3.15 The framebuffer, the memory type and the memory budget

**3.15.1** The framebuffer is mapped with the uncached memory type, or the processor's writes sit in the data cache while the video device scans out stale memory; a `FENCE` separates writing a device's registers from setting its go bit. *(D)*

**3.15.2** On the first board the block RAM (about 5.5 megabits on the DE10-Nano) cannot hold a modern frame (a 1920 by 1080 frame at 32 bits is 8.3 MB, and at the 48-bit pixel of 3.10.2 it is 12.4 MB), so the framebuffer lives in main memory and the block RAM holds the scan-out line buffer of a few kilobytes. *(D)*

**3.15.3** The block RAM budget (caches, translation buffer, the blitter's scratch and the sound voices' state) is settled before the video device is built. *(D)*

**3.15.4** The framebuffer is ordinary memory and the devices' registers are device windows (section 2.3.6), so a page-table entry that maps the framebuffer as a device, or a register page as cached memory, is a mistake that the memory type of the page makes visible to the kernel. *(P)*

*Commentary.* **Trade-offs.** An uncached framebuffer makes a single pixel write slow and a long run of writes no slower per byte, which suits the blitter (it writes the bus directly) and punishes pixel-by-pixel drawing from the processor, which is what a blitter is for. **Modern comparables.** Write-combining memory types on x86 and ARM serve the same purpose. **Impact.** *Kernel:* the mapping rule is one memory-type value in the page-table entry. *Compiler:* none. *Hardware:* the scan-out reads main memory by bus mastering, which competes with the processor for bandwidth.

## 3.16 virtio-gpu: the standard graphics device

The first iteration's display (errata 79: "virtio is fine"; virtio-gpu in its 2D mode) is a virtio device like the disk of chapter 2, so everything of 2.6 applies: it is found by enumeration, its queues are rings of descriptors in memory, and the work is commands in a queue. What the commands are, in the Linux header `virtio_gpu.h` and as the QEMU emulator (`hw/display/virtio-gpu.c`) carries them out, is this.

**The vocabulary.** A **resource** is a picture that the device owns, named by a number the driver chooses (`resource_id`), with a pixel format, a width and a height. Its **backing** is the driver's own memory that holds the pixels: a list of `{addr, length}` entries (`struct virtio_gpu_mem_entry`) naming the pages. A **scanout** is one output of the device (up to `VIRTIO_GPU_MAX_SCANOUTS`, 16, in the header); `SET_SCANOUT` says which resource, and which rectangle of it, an output shows. Two commands move and show the pixels: `TRANSFER_TO_HOST_2D` copies a rectangle of the backing memory into the device's picture, and `RESOURCE_FLUSH` tells the device that a rectangle changed and should be shown. The pixel formats are 32 bits with 8 bits a channel: `B8G8R8A8_UNORM`, `B8G8R8X8_UNORM`, `A8R8G8B8_UNORM`, `X8R8G8B8_UNORM`, `R8G8B8A8_UNORM`, `X8B8G8R8_UNORM`, `A8B8G8R8_UNORM`, `R8G8B8X8_UNORM` (values 1 to 4, 67, 68, 121 and 134 in the header; the names say the order of the channels, which is the *colour order* annoyance of 3.9). Every command starts with a `virtio_gpu_ctrl_hdr`: a type, flags, a 64-bit fence id, a context id and a ring index; the flag `VIRTIO_GPU_FLAG_FENCE` asks that the response carry the fence id once the work is done, which is the device's only completion-ordering tool. The rectangle is `struct virtio_gpu_rect`: x, y, width, height, four 32-bit numbers.

![The virtio-gpu command sequence](figures/gfx-virtio-seq.svg)

```
# the whole of a virtio-gpu 2D start-up and one update, as pseudocode over the queue of chapter 2.6
send(RESOURCE_CREATE_2D,     resource_id=1, format=X8R8G8B8, width=1024, height=768)
send(RESOURCE_ATTACH_BACKING, resource_id=1, entries=[{addr=PAGES, length=1024*768*4}])
send(SET_SCANOUT,            scanout_id=0, resource_id=1, rect={0,0,1024,768})

draw_into(PAGES)                                       # the driver writes pixels: ordinary stores
send(TRANSFER_TO_HOST_2D,    resource_id=1, rect={0,0,1024,768}, offset=0)
send(RESOURCE_FLUSH,         resource_id=1, rect={0,0,1024,768})
```

What QEMU's code does (read in `virtio-gpu.c` here): `transfer_to_host_2d` rejects a rectangle that is not inside the resource with `VIRTIO_GPU_RESP_ERR_INVALID_PARAMETER`, then copies it **row by row** from the backing into the resource, using the resource's stride, or in one copy if the rectangle spans whole rows; `resource_flush` rejects a rectangle outside the resource in the same way and then asks each scanout showing the resource to update the part of its display that the rectangle covers. So the transfer is the **upload** and the flush is the **present**, and the program must do both after drawing.

**What it does not define.** Neither the header nor the QEMU function names anything about the vertical blank, a page flip, a refresh rate or tearing: the flush says "show this rectangle now" and the host decides when its own display next reads it. The only timing handle is the fence of the header. The emulator's configuration structure has a `refresh_rate` field for each output that a host can set (`virtio-gpu.h`), and the device can report an EDID with the optional feature `VIRTIO_GPU_F_EDID`; the header does not say that either of them changes how a flush is timed. This chapter does not claim more than that. The standard device therefore gives a program the damage rectangle (the flush rectangle), a cursor (the cursor queue and its `UPDATE_CURSOR` and `MOVE_CURSOR`), a fence and display information (`GET_DISPLAY_INFO`: the size and enabled flag of each output), and none of the anti-tearing sugar of 3.8 beyond that.

**3.16.1** The standard graphics device of the first iteration is virtio-gpu in 2D mode over the virtio PCI Express transport (errata 79); its commands are the six of the figure, plus the cursor queue and the display-information query. *(D)*

**3.16.2** The feature subset of virtio-gpu that the pdp64 requires (the EDID feature, the fence flag, the cursor queue, the blob and 3D features of the header, which are not used for 2D) is not specified. *(S)*

**3.16.3** The framebuffer of virtio-gpu is the backing memory that the driver allocates; on the pdp64 it is ordinary memory mapped uncached (3.15.1), and the transfer command is the hand-off that tells the device to read it (the five steps of 2.5.3). *(P)*

**3.16.4** The standard device has no 48-bit pixel and no text layer: the 48-bit pixel of 3.10.2 belongs to the native device that is deferred, and a text terminal over virtio-gpu is a program that draws glyphs into the backing memory with the processor (the way 3.5 ends) or with a library. *(S)*

*Commentary.* **Precedent.** Every virtualised PC uses virtio-gpu or a card of the same shape: the guest draws into its own memory and tells the host which rectangle to show. **Trade-offs.** The route is the most standard (one driver, tested, the same in QEMU, `sim64` and the FPGA, as errata 79 says) and does everything in software; the processor writes every pixel and the host copies them, which for a terminal is cheap if the damage is small and for a game is the very cost of 3.5. **Improvements.** The sugar of 3.9 can be added as a library over it (a library that sets up the six commands, allocates the picture, draws text from a glyph cache and flushes only the damage), which is sketch 4 of 3.21. **Benefits.** No special hardware; the display of the first iteration works in the emulator now. **Modern comparables.** Linux's `virtio_gpu` driver, which sits on the DRM interface of 3.9. **Impact.** *Kernel:* a driver for the six commands, one queue of chapter 2.6, and a cursor queue. *Compiler:* none. *Hardware:* none beyond the virtio device and the bus.

## 3.17 A standard graphics card

**3.17.1** A PCI Express graphics card is not excluded by the architecture: a bridge could present PCI Express functions as slots on the bus, enumerated like any other device. *(P)*

**3.17.2** Against a standard card: PCI Express configuration space, base-address mapping and message-signalled interrupts, a driver stack for a programming model that for some vendors must be reverse-engineered, the card's firmware and the initialisation a PC's firmware performs, and no hard PCI Express block on the first board (the Cyclone V and the Zynq 7020 lack one; a Zynq UltraScale+ has it). *(D)*

**3.17.3** On the other side PCI Express is a standard enumerated bus, which suits the bus-as-a-device design (each function a slot, as each USB device is), and OpenBSD carries graphics drivers for several vendors' cards ported from Linux *(not confirmed in the sources read)*. *(P)*

*Commentary.* **Trade-offs.** The terminal described here works on the first board and costs a few thousand lookup tables; a standard card needs a root complex, which is a large block when it is not already on the chip, and a graphics driver ported to the pdp64, which is a larger task than the architecture. **Modern comparables.** PowerPC and RISC-V workstations plug in PCI Express graphics cards with open drivers. **Impact.** *Kernel:* a PCI Express card would need a large graphics driver ported. *Compiler:* none. *Hardware:* a PCI Express root complex and bridge to the bus.

## 3.18 A c64 mode: a small computer on the die that acts as the terminal

The author, 2026-11-10: "we will have a c64 mode, where a c128 is built onto the die with sid etc, so a bit of code loaded into its address space by pcie dma will let it act as a terminal the way the blit and vt55 did." This is a **proposed design idea**, not a design: it says what the author would like to have and leaves every question about it open, and this section writes it up for the two readers and states those questions.

**What the Blit did.** The Blit (3.3) was a terminal with a computer of its own, a Motorola 68000 (8 MHz, general knowledge of the repository's records), and the host did not send it escape codes to interpret: it sent it **programs**. The records of the repository (`docs/kernel-pain-points.md` section 14I, from the Blit papers) say that the terminal received downloaded code over its serial line, that the terminal software `mpxterm` (1,627 lines of C and 204 of assembler, as recorded) multiplexed several host programs over the one line, and that the authors' reason was that "an intelligent terminal is not an idiot savant, it is one that can be educated". So a **terminal with its own small computer** means this: the display, the keyboard and the mouse are driven by a processor on the terminal's side of the cable, which runs a program that the host loaded; the host talks to that program by messages, and the program does the drawing. How the download worked in detail (the loading protocol, the format of a program) is not in the records and is *unverified* here. The VT52 and VT100 were the opposite, a fixed circuit that obeyed a fixed set of codes (3.2); and "vt55" is, as 3.2 says, the author's naming.

**Why a small core with the C64's chips is a terminal on a chip.** Take a small computer, give it the C64 family's display and sound circuits, the **VIC** (the video chip: text cells, sprites, colours), the **SID** (the sound chip), **screen memory**, **colour RAM**, **glyph memory** (3.11) and a window towards the host, and let the host load its program: that is a terminal, with every part of it in one place. The program on the small core is the terminal's firmware: it receives bytes or messages from the host (by the mailbox), puts characters into screen memory, plays a sound, moves a sprite. The host's operating system sees a device that takes characters, as it saw a teletype, and the author's old problem (3.2) is solved the Blit's way: the cleverness is in a program that the host can replace, and the pdp64 core is not burdened with the drawing.

**What "loaded by PCI Express DMA into its address space" means, mechanically.** The host places the program image (a block of bytes, the terminal's code and its initial data) in its own memory. It tells the device's **DMA engine** (the part that copies memory without the processor's help, chapter 2) the address of the image, its length and the address in the small core's memory where it is to go. The engine reads the host's memory over the PCI Express link (the bus-master read of 2.5) and writes the bytes into the core's memory. The core has been held in **reset** (a line that keeps a processor from running until it is released) since the machine started, so that it executes nothing while its memory is incomplete; when the copy is done the host releases the reset, and the core starts at its fixed start address. In pseudocode, with placeholder names (no register of any such device exists):

```
load_terminal(image, length):                     # on the host, as a driver would
    poke(CORE_CTRL, HOLD_RESET)                   # the core is stopped
    poke(DMA_SRC,  physical_address_of(image))    # where the image is, in host memory
    poke(DMA_DST,  CORE_LOAD_ADDRESS)             # where it must go, in the core's address space
    poke(DMA_LEN,  length)
    poke(DMA_GO,   1)
    wait_until(peek(DMA_DONE) == 1)               # or an interrupt
    poke(CORE_CTRL, RELEASE_RESET)                # the core starts executing the loaded code
    # the host now talks to it through the mailbox:
    mailbox_send(PUT_TEXT, row, col, "hello")     # a message the loaded program understands
```

![A small core as the terminal: the loaded program and its address space](figures/gfx-core-map.svg)

The picture of the core's address space is the C64's (3.4) with a mailbox added. Everything the loaded program needs is in one flat space, as in the 1982 machine: its own code, the screen and colour memory, glyph slots, the video and sound registers, and a window that the host also sees. Video goes out to the display connector and audio to its audio channel (3.14); the sound path to HDMI audio brings the audio clock relation of 3.14.4 with it, which the small core's program would have to honour or the transmitter's own calculation would have to supply.

**3.18.1** A **c64 mode** may be offered: a small core on the die, with the C64 family's circuits (SID and others), into whose address space a program is loaded by DMA over PCI Express so that it acts as a terminal, as the Blit's terminal program did. *(P)* (The author, 2026-11-10, errata 105.)

**3.18.2** C64 mode is the enabling or disabling of the on-die c128 as a PCI Express device: when it is enabled it takes over the graphics and audio output, or sends them to a compositor; the form of the compositor path is open. *(P)* (The author, 2026-11-10: "the mode is just enabling/disabling the c128 as a pcie device so it takes over the graphics and audio output... or sends them to a compositor. not sure what that would look like. is there virtio-compositor?")

**3.18.3** Everything about this idea other than the two sentences above is open and unspecified: which core; whether "c64 mode" also means running unmodified C64 software; how video memory is shared; the form of the compositor path; how the core and the host talk; how the loaded image is authenticated and contained; and how the idea relates to the native devices and to virtio. *(S)*

**The questions the author's words leave open**, stated and not answered:

1. **Which core?** He wrote "a c128". The C128 (general knowledge, *unverified* here) combined an 8502 (a faster member of the 6502 family, like the C64's 6510), a Z80 (a second, unrelated processor, used for CP/M), the VIC-IIe (the video chip with extra registers), the 8563 **VDC** (a separate 80-column video chip, with its own memory) and a SID, with a memory-management chip for banking. The repository's records mention the 8502 and not the Z80 or the VDC: the author's earlier device chat (`docs/c64-devices-from-chat.md` section 4, chat-era, nothing decided) put an **"8502 compatibility island"** in the design: a soft 8502 held in reset at power-on, with its registers readable and writable by the host, running at the C64's clock, so that Commodore software could run. The questions: is it (a) a real 6502-family core, as a hard block or as a soft core in the programmable logic; (b) an *emulation* of one, in software or in hardware; or (c) a core of the pdp64's own instruction set given the C64's registers? Does "c128" mean the 8502 only, or the Z80, the VDC and the memory-management chip too?
2. **What does "c64 mode" mean, beyond the author's clarification?** He says the mode is "just" the on-die c128 appearing as a PCI Express device, taking over the graphics and audio output or sending them to a compositor (3.18.2). Still open: does the loaded program run **unmodified C64 software** (the Kernal, BASIC, games, which need the exact chips and timing: the "island" idea of the chat) or only **terminal firmware** written for the small core? And what is the compositor path (below)?
3. **How is video memory shared** with the host and with virtio-gpu (3.16)? Is the core's screen memory its own, behind a window (VRAM in the sense of 3.21), or in host memory that the video chip reads by DMA (3.15.2)? How does the core's picture reach the display when the standard device is virtio-gpu, which has a resource of its own, and who owns the scanout?
4. **How do the core and the host talk?** A mailbox and a doorbell (registers and a write that interrupts), a virtio device with queues (as everything else, 2.6), or a shared window of memory that both can read and write? The figure draws a mailbox only as a placeholder.
5. **How is the host's program image authenticated and contained?** A program loaded from the host runs on the die with access to the core's whole address space and, depending on the design, to the bus. The IOMMU of errata 75 and 85 (the table of which memory a device may touch) is the architecture's answer for devices that master the bus; whether the small core's DMA goes through it, whether the image must be signed, and who holds the signing key, are the author's decision and are listed here only as questions.
6. **How does it relate to the deferred native devices (`VIC-64X`, `SID-64X`, `BLT64`, errata 78) and to virtio across the board (errata 95)?** The tension, stated plainly and not resolved: the author said "forget the native 8 register devices. gone. virtio across the board." (errata 95) and, earlier, that "iommu will allow us to map in c64/c128 and i think we should, but that is extraneous" (errata 75, 2026-11-03). A small core with the C64 family's chips is a native, register-level device by definition, so it is either (i) the surviving form of the deferred native devices, serving as a terminal, with the VIC and SID as its private chips and not as the system's devices; (ii) something that speaks virtio to the host on its outside (a virtio device served by the core's program) and the C64's registers only on its inside, which would honour errata 95 on the bus; or (iii) a thing apart from the standard, an optional extra that errata 95 did not have in view. Which is meant is the author's.
7. **What does it cost** in gates and die area? A 6502-class core is a few thousand gates (general knowledge, *unverified*); the SID and VIC, the memories and the DMA engine are what the FPGA's block RAM (3.15.2) must also hold; **an estimate, mine, unmeasured**: the core itself is small beside the memory of the display, which is why the idea is attractive. A hard block on the die costs area for everyone; a soft core exists only in the programmable logic of the FPGA phase.
8. **How does its sound reach HDMI audio?** Through the same 48-bit-sample sound path (errata 74, 3.10.3) or the SID's own output converted to the audio stream of 3.14.4.

**The options, with their trade-offs, none decided.** (a) **A hard core on the die** (a real 6502-family core plus the chips): fastest and smallest per core; permanent die area, and it fixes the idea in silicon before the author has answered the questions above. (b) **A soft core in the FPGA phase only**: costs nothing in silicon and lets the idea be tried on the first board (the programmable logic can hold a small core and the chips, as the MiSTer framework does, 3.14); gone from a later chip unless it is made hard. (c) **Software emulation of the C64 machine on a pdp64 core** (the pdp64 runs an emulator of the 6502, VIC and SID as a program): no hardware, easiest to change, uses a core and loses the C64's timing exactness; it is the Blit's idea turned around, the emulator being the "terminal program". (d) **None, and the terminal is a virtio device served by a host process**: the standard route (errata 95), the terminal's firmware is a program on the host (as a terminal emulator is, 3.7); nothing on the die; it loses the property that the terminal is a computer of its own, which the author values in the Blit. Option (c) and option (d) differ in where the C64 machine runs, not in whether it exists.

### The compositor question

The author, 2026-11-10: "the mode is just enabling/disabling the c128 as a pcie device so it takes over the graphics and audio output... or sends them to a compositor. not sure what that would look like. is there virtio-compositor?"

**What a compositor is.** A **compositor** is the program (or the hardware) that takes the pictures of several windows or sources and combines them into the one picture that is sent to the display, which is then scanned out row by row as in 3.8, with its own flipping. A desktop's window system is one: each program draws into its own picture (its **surface**) and the compositor places the pictures, one over another, into the frame that the scanout reads. In 3.5 the windowing systems did it in software; in a sketch with layers (3.21, sketches 1 and 2) the scanout hardware does it, as the C64's chip did with sprites.

**Why "takes over" is the simple case.** If the c128 owns the output outright when it is enabled, there is nothing to compose: the machine has one scanout and one audio path, and the question is only who drives them. The multiplexer is a single enable bit (path (a) below), and switching the bit is the whole of the mode change. The compositor case is harder in exactly one way: the core is no longer the owner of the display but one client among others, so its picture and its sound must be handed over in some form that the host's compositor, or the display controller's layers, can place.

![Five data paths for a c64-mode core sharing the display](figures/gfx-compositor-paths.svg)

**What exists, checked.** There is **no virtio device called a compositor**: the device types listed in `virtio_ids.h` on this machine are network, block, console, random numbers, balloon, SCSI, 9p, GPU (16), clock, input (18), socket, crypto, IOMMU, memory, sound (25), file system, persistent memory, video encoder and decoder, I2C, watchdog, CAN, DMA buffer (37), Bluetooth, GPIO and SPI, with a few others (network wireless, firmware and security modules), and the standard headers on this machine are `virtio_9p`, `balloon`, `blk`, `bt`, `console`, `crypto`, `fs`, `gpio`, `gpu`, `i2c`, `input`, `iommu`, `mem`, `net`, `pmem`, `rng`, `rtc`, `scmi`, `scsi`, `snd`, `spi` and `vsock`, among the transport files. What virtio-gpu offers that comes near a compositor, in `virtio_gpu.h`: up to 16 scanouts (`VIRTIO_GPU_MAX_SCANOUTS`), so the host can present several outputs; `SET_SCANOUT_BLOB`, which sets a scanout from a blob resource (a memory object shared with the host without copying); a **cursor queue** separate from the control queue, with `UPDATE_CURSOR` and `MOVE_CURSOR`; capability sets (`VIRTIO_GPU_CAPSET_VIRGL`, `VIRGL2` and `GFXSTREAM_VULKAN`) selected with `context_init` so that several rendering contexts, each with its own ordering (the "timelines" of the header comment), share the device. QEMU's documentation (`docs/system/devices/virtio/virtio-gpu.rst`) describes a variant, `virtio-gpu-rutabaga`, that provides "gfxstream rendering and Wayland display passthrough", with a capset named `cross-domain` for Linux guests and an experimental `x-gfxstream-composer` for Android guests; it says no more than that about what the cross-domain path carries. What I take from it, as general knowledge and *unverified* beyond those sentences: the crosvm project's use of the same mechanism so that a guest's windows appear as windows of the host's Wayland compositor, which is the path (c) below; Wayland is the Linux display protocol in which clients hand their surfaces to a compositor. **virtio-snd** and **virtio-input** exist for audio and for the keyboard and mouse. So **path (b) and path (c) are built from parts that exist**, and **path (e) is a new device type**: the process by which a virtio device type gets its number is the specification's own, and is *unverified* here.

**The options, none decided.**

* **(a) Exclusive takeover.** When the c128 is enabled it owns scanout 0 and the audio path; the host's picture and sound stop. *Gives:* the simplest hardware (an enable bit and a mute); the C64's one-screen model. *Costs:* the host cannot show anything while the core runs; a text terminal that wants to coexist with a window system cannot. Right for a console in the Blit's sense, wrong for a window.
* **(b) The core is one more virtio-gpu scanout and one virtio-snd stream.** It is a second head of the display device (or a window on the host's, if the host maps heads to windows) and one more stream of the sound device. *Gives:* nothing new to invent; the host's existing drivers and compositor handle it; it is the shape of errata 95. *Costs:* the core must speak the virtio-gpu and virtio-snd protocols, so a small core would need those in firmware or hardware, which is the larger part of a driver.
* **(c) A surface for the host's compositor (the author's stated preference, below).** The core renders into a resource (or a blob) that the host's compositor treats as a window's surface, as any client's. *Gives:* the core appears as a window, with the host's window management, overlap and flipping; the path that Wayland passthrough takes (general knowledge, unverified). *Costs:* the compositor must know that the surface is the core's; a copy or a shared blob; the core's frame timing becomes the compositor's.
* **(d) Layers at scanout** (sketch 1 of 3.21). The display controller has a layer for the core and a layer for the host's picture and composes them as it reads them out. *Gives:* the cheapest path and no copy; the core's picture is a plane in video memory; a vertical-blank latch per layer (3.8) switches it without a tear. *Costs:* the display controller must be the layered one of sketch 1, which is not the first-iteration device.
* **(e) A new virtio device type for compositing.** It does not exist. What it would need to be useful: a way for a client to give the device a surface (a resource or blob), to say where it goes and how it overlaps, to swap it atomically at a blank, to ask for the frame's timing and to hand over the matching audio. Most of those are what virtio-gpu's resources, scanouts and fences already say; *a new device type would repeat them*, and it is the author's call whether a compositor is a device or a protocol of existing ones. *Gives:* one model for every client of the display, the core included. *Costs:* a specification to write and have accepted (unverified: the virtio process), and a driver for every host.

**3.18.4** Of the five compositor paths the author prefers (c), that the c128 renders into a buffer that the host's compositor treats as a surface: "c128 drawing into a buffer makes most sense" (2026-11-10, errata 106). A stated preference, and not a decision. *(P)*

*The author's leaning.* He prefers path (c): the c128 draws into a buffer, and the host compositor places it. It is the path that makes the core a client like any other, the one the rio design takes for every window (3.19: a window is a buffer and a rectangle), and the one whose parts exist (a virtio-gpu resource or blob as the surface, 3.16); what remains open under it is how the compositor learns that the buffer is the core's, who owns the copy or the shared memory, and how the core's frame timing meets the compositor's. The audio form of the same choice is in 3.20.

**How the switch avoids tearing and pops.** Switching the mode (enable or disable) changes who feeds the scanout and the audio path. If it changes in the middle of a frame the picture tears (3.8) and the sound clicks (a sudden change of the waveform's value is heard as a pop). The remedy has the shape of 3.8: make the hand-over at a **vertical blank**, as the latch of 3.10.9 does for registers (the enable bit as one more shadowed register that `ARM` latches), and **mute with a short ramp** (a fade of a few milliseconds down before and up after, rather than a cut). Which of these a design uses depends on the path: in (a) the enable bit is the shadowed register and the mute belongs to the audio path; in (b) to (e) the compositor does the hand-over and the core only stops or starts its stream. The audio clock relation of 3.14.4 must be unchanged across the switch, or the transmitter loses its lock and the sound drops.

*Commentary.* **Precedent.** The Blit loaded its terminal program from the host (above); the C128 put several complete computers in one box (general knowledge, *unverified*); the MiSTer framework runs C64 and C128 cores in programmable logic; a PC's graphics card carries its own processor and firmware (*unverified*). **Trade-offs.** A computer of its own makes the terminal programmable and independent of the host's drawing, and makes it a second machine to load, secure and keep consistent with the first. **Impact.** *Kernel:* a driver for the loader and the mailbox. *Compiler:* none. *Hardware:* a core, memory, DMA, the C64 family's circuits; or nothing, in option (d).

## 3.19 Windows: from the Blit to rio and NeWS

The author, 2026-11-10: "it should mention blit terminal was inspiration for the rio and NeWS windowing systems, and we'd like to bring over what we can of that simplicity also."

**What the sources confirm and what they do not.** That the Blit was the inspiration of **rio** is borne out by the papers recorded in `docs/kernel-pain-points.md` section 14I: the Blit ran `mux`, a window system in the terminal; its successor on Plan 9 was **8½**, whose paper says "Primarily for reasons of history and familiarity, the general model and appearance of 8½ are similar to those of mux", and **rio** replaced 8½ ("Note that 8½ has been replaced by rio (see rio(1))"; rio(1): "Rio manages asynchronous layers of text, or windows, on a raster display"; the word *layers* is the Blit's). That the Blit inspired **NeWS** is the author's statement; **no source read for the repository makes that claim**, and the glossary says so, so here it is the author's account, not a checked fact. What the repository does record about NeWS is from *The UNIX-HATERS Handbook* (section 14G of the same file): NeWS was an extensible window server in which "the client can download programs and data structures to draw the whole schematic", and "ultimately, NeWS was not economically or politically viable" (the quoted reasons are about licensing). That NeWS was Sun's, that its programs were written in PostScript (a page-description language that is also a programming language) and that James Gosling designed it are general knowledge, *unverified* here.

**What rio is, for the two readers.** rio is the window system of Plan 9 (the research operating system that followed Unix at Bell Labs; Rob Pike wrote 8½ and its descendant). Its central idea, as the 8½ paper records it, is that the window system is a **file server**: "The most important contributor to its small size, though, is its overall design as a file server." A program uses a window by opening files (`/dev/cons` for text, `/dev/mouse`, `/dev/bitblt` or later `/dev/draw` for graphics): each window is a private screen that the program draws on, and the server keeps the window's bitmap and carries out the drawing for the program ("no bitmap graphics operations are executed in the client at all"). There is one small server and no **toolkit** (a library of buttons, menus and other widgets that every program of a window system links in; X and its descendants have several, rio has none in the base system). The result, in the paper's words, is a window system of a size that a programmer can read in an afternoon (the paper says "small"; I give no line count).

**What NeWS was, for the two readers.** In a conventional window system a program on the host sends the display server drawing commands one at a time over a slow link, and each round trip costs time (the "latency" complaint of 3.2). NeWS made the server itself programmable: the host *downloaded a program* into the display server, and the server ran it locally for every mouse movement, so that the interactive loop (dragging a shape across the screen) did not cross the link. This is the Blit's idea one step on: the Blit downloaded a program into a *terminal*; NeWS downloaded programs into a *window server*. The repository's own comparison (`kernel-pain-points.md` row 10 and its note) says the Handbook's argument "is the same shape as the Blit lineage", and notes that it does not claim a historical link beyond what the sources say.

**3.19.1** The pdp64 is to bring over what it can of the simplicity of the Blit's descendants rio and NeWS (the author: "we'd like to bring over what we can of that simplicity also"); a goal, and the means are open. *(P)*

**3.19.2** Which of the options below the pdp64 takes, if any, is open. *(S)*

**What simplicity could be carried over, as options with their trade-offs, none decided.**

* **A window is a plain buffer and a rectangle.** The program owns a pixel buffer (or a cell array, 3.11) and the system knows only where to place it. *Gives:* the model of 3.9's goal; drawing is writes to memory with nothing to ask for. *Costs:* a buffer per window (memory), and a rule for who may write whose (the memory protection of chapter 6). *Ties to:* path (c) of the compositor question (3.18), where the c128 draws into a buffer that the compositor places.
* **Drawing is writes to that buffer.** No drawing protocol in the base system: the program uses `memcpy`, a library, or the blitter (3.21 sketch 2). *Gives:* no round trips, no server-side drawing language. *Costs:* every program draws for itself (the opposite of rio's "the server carries out the drawing"); a library must exist for text and shapes.
* **The server composes the buffers.** A small program (or the display hardware, sketch 1) places the buffers into the one picture. *Gives:* one place that handles overlap, flip and tear (3.8). *Costs:* a copy or a layer per window; the server must be trusted with every buffer.
* **Downloadable server-side code.** A program is loaded into the display server, as the Blit's terminal program and NeWS's PostScript were: here, into the c128 core of 3.18, which would be the display server. *Gives:* the display can be given new abilities without changing the host; interaction stays local. *Costs:* a second program to secure (3.18 question 5), and the two-halves problem of the Blit ("writing one program in two pieces is much harder than writing two programs", `kernel-pain-points.md` row 12).
* **No toolkit in the base system.** The system provides windows, text and pixels; widgets, if any, are a library the program chooses. *Gives:* the size rio had; nothing to keep consistent. *Costs:* every program draws its own look, and users see the inconsistency.
* **Windows as files, or as virtio queues.** rio exposes a window as files; the pdp64's devices are virtio queues (chapter 2). A window could be a queue of drawing messages (one more virtio shape), or a file the kernel maps, or both. *Gives:* the uniformity rio and 8½ had ("the same device interface at every level"). *Costs:* a naming and permission model for files (a chapter 6 matter) or a queue per window.

## 3.20 The audio path: samples, mixers and the pdp64

The author asked, 2026-11-10: "what is the audio equivalent, what do those mixers do to combine multiple programs audio?" The answer is that audio has the same structure as pixels, with a **mixer** where video has a compositor, and this section takes the parts in the same order as 3.8 and 3.18.

**What a sound card does.** A sound is a pressure wave, stored as a list of **samples** (numbers, each the height of the wave at one instant) taken at a fixed rate: 44,100 or 48,000 a second are the usual rates (44.1 kHz is the compact disc's and 48 kHz the video world's; general knowledge, *unverified*, though both appear in the virtio-snd rate list, below), with each sample a 16-bit integer, or 24 bits, or a floating-point number. A **DAC** (digital-to-analogue converter: the chip that turns a number into a voltage) takes one sample on each tick of a clock, and the speaker moves. The device is fed from a **ring buffer**: a block of memory, treated as a circle, which the program fills and the device empties, the device reading the next sample at each tick and the program writing ahead of it. It is the scanout idea of 3.8 exactly: the display reads the framebuffer at a fixed rate, the DAC reads the ring buffer at a fixed rate; a **buffer underrun** (the device reaches samples the program has not yet written) is **audio's tearing**, heard as a click or a gap, and is as sure to happen when the program is late as a tear is when it draws late.

```
# fill the ring buffer: the program writes ahead of the read pointer, and notices an underrun
fill_ring(ring, next_frame):                 # called by an interrupt each period, or in a loop
    while free_space(ring) >= PERIOD:
        samples = generate_or_read(PERIOD)   # this period's samples
        write_to_ring(ring, samples)
    if device_read_pointer_passed_write_pointer(ring):    # underrun
        count_underrun(); write_silence_up_to(...)        # a click is unavoidable; at least not noise
```

**The SID's way.** The C64 did not store samples at all (3.4): the SID has three **voices**, each an oscillator with a waveform, an envelope and a filter, in hardware, programmed by registers: a program says "this frequency, this waveform, this attack and release" and the chip *synthesises* the sound. The sound is never in memory as numbers; there is nothing to underrun and nothing to mix in software, because the chip mixes its three voices and its filter inside itself. It is exactly the difference between the text mode of 3.4 (the chip draws from a description) and a framebuffer (the program draws every dot).

**What a mixer does.** When several programs each play sound, the card has one ring buffer and one DAC, so something must combine their sounds into the one stream: the **mixer**, "the audio compositor". Its job, per **period** (a short block of samples, a few milliseconds):

```
mix_period(streams, out):                    # one block of the output; runs once per period
    for each frame i in 0 .. PERIOD-1:
        sum = 0
        for s in streams:
            x = s.next_sample()              # after converting s to the output's rate and format
            sum += x * s.volume              # per-stream volume
        out[i] = clip(sum)                   # or a limiter: a gentle gain reduction instead of a hard cut
    write_to_ring(out)
```

It must also **convert each stream to a common sample rate and format**, because one program plays 44.1 kHz music, another 48 kHz speech, a third floating-point effects; this is **sample-rate conversion** (resampling: computing the samples of a wave at the new rate), a filtering job, and it is why a mixer is not just an adder. It **applies per-stream volume**, and **clips or limits** the sum, because two loud samples added exceed what the output can hold (**clipping**: a hard cut of the top of the wave, heard as distortion; a **limiter** reduces the gain smoothly to avoid it). There is a **latency against underrun** trade-off: a small buffer means the sound follows the program's action quickly (low latency) and leaves little margin for the program being late; a large one is safe and late. And there is **clock drift**: the program's idea of time and the DAC's clock are different crystals, so over minutes one runs ahead of the other; the remedy is for the mixer to be driven by the *device's* clock (it writes when the ring buffer has room, not when the program's timer says) and to resample slightly (a fraction of a percent) to absorb the difference.

**Who the mixer is in real systems** (general knowledge, *unverified* except where noted): on Linux a **sound server** (PulseAudio and, more recently, PipeWire) or ALSA's software mixing plug-in (`dmix`); on macOS CoreAudio; on Windows WASAPI's shared mode. On this machine there is a `pulseaudio` program and the ALSA library (`libasound`) installed; PipeWire is not. QEMU's own audio has a mixing engine (`audio/mixeng.c`, with `audio-mixeng-be.c`) and backends for ALSA, PulseAudio, PipeWire, CoreAudio, DirectSound, JACK and SDL (the file names are in `qemu-src/audio`): a virtual machine's sound is mixed by the **host**, as it is for any other program of the host.

**What virtio-snd defines** (checked in `/usr/include/linux/virtio_snd.h`). Four queues: control, event, transmit (`TX`) and receive (`RX`). Streams are **PCM streams** (pulse-code modulation: plain samples) with a direction (output or input), described by `virtio_snd_pcm_info`: a bitmap of formats (from 4-bit ADPCM and 8-bit mu-law through `S16`, `S24`, `S32`, `FLOAT` and `FLOAT64` to DSD and IEC958), a bitmap of rates (5,512 Hz to 384,000 Hz, including 44,100 and 48,000), and a range of channels. The driver sets a stream's parameters (`PCM_SET_PARAMS`: `buffer_bytes`, `period_bytes`, channels, format, rate), prepares, starts and stops it, then sends audio as buffers on the TX queue, each tagged with a `stream_id` and answered with a `virtio_snd_pcm_status` that carries a `status` and the **current device latency** in bytes. The event queue reports `PCM_PERIOD_ELAPSED` and `PCM_XRUN` (an underrun or overrun: the same word as the ring buffer's), and jack events; the feature bits include shared-memory modes, polling and period and xrun events. Controls (volume and the like) are optional (`VIRTIO_SND_F_CTLS`). What the header says about **who mixes**: nothing; it defines one stream at a time, the way the device presents *one card*, and the mixing of several programs happens on the host side of the device (in QEMU's engine above), or, in a machine with no host, in the pdp64's own kernel or library before the stream is sent. The pdp64 *is* that machine, so who mixes is the pdp64's open question.

**3.20.1** The standard audio device is virtio-snd (errata 79); the device carries one stream at a time as a PCM stream of the formats and rates the device lists. *(D)*

**3.20.2** The pdp64's mixer (the program or device that combines the audio of several programs into one stream, converts rates and formats and applies volume), where it lives (the kernel, a library or the device) and who drives it, are unspecified. *(S)*

**The audio of the c128 core, the options, parallel to the pixel options of 3.18, none decided.** The author's stated preference for the pixel path (the c128 draws into a buffer, 3.18) has an audio form, and it is a natural pairing, but the author has stated no preference for audio.

* **Exclusive takeover of the audio path** (parallel to 3.18 (a)): when the core is enabled it owns the audio output and the host's sound stops. *Gives:* nothing to mix; the SID's output goes to the converter or the HDMI audio channel. *Costs:* no music while the terminal bells.
* **The SID's output as a virtio-snd stream** that the host mixes with the others (parallel to 3.18 (c)): the core renders its voices to samples (or the SID model does), and the samples go into a stream as any program's would. *Gives:* the core is a client of the mixer; the host's volume, rate conversion and routing apply. *Costs:* the SID must produce samples at a rate (a design choice: the 48-bit sample path of errata 74 is exactly that), and its latency is the stream's.
* **A mix inside the device with a fixed gain:** the core's voices and the host's stream are summed by a small mixer in the device, at a fixed or registered gain. *Gives:* nothing for the host to know; cheap (an adder and a clamp). *Costs:* a fixed policy; the device has its own clock and a rate converter, or both streams must already be at one rate.
* **Hand-over with a fade:** whichever is chosen, switching the core on or off ramps the volume over a few milliseconds and changes at a period boundary (3.18) so that no pop is heard.

## 3.21 A design still being worked out: what a mashup of GPU, video memory, C64 and Blit could look like

The author, 2026-11-10: "conclude the graphics chapter: we're still figuring it out, what would a mashup of gpu, vram, c64 and blit look like." **Nothing in this section is decided.** Every statement is tagged *(P)* or *(S)*; the four sketches are the shapes of ideas, put side by side so that the questions at the end can be answered, and none is recommended by the repository.

**Two terms first.** **VRAM** (video memory) is memory that belongs to the display device: it sits on the device, behind a window on the bus, and the scanout reads it without crossing the bus; the processor reaches it through the window (slowly: every access is a bus transaction). The alternative is **system memory** that the device reads by DMA as a bus master, as the video device of 3.15.2 does; the framebuffer is then ordinary memory and the scanout competes with the processor for it. A GPU card has VRAM; the first board of the pdp64 has none to speak of (the block RAM is 5.5 megabits and a frame is 8.3 MB, 3.15.2), so on it every layer lives in system memory, and "VRAM" in the sketches below means "memory the device owns the use of", whichever kind it is. **Compositing** is combining several pictures into the one that is shown; the C64's sprites over text over a background were compositing in hardware, as the scanout read them out.

The properties the author has asked for, which each sketch is measured against: **(T)** simple peek/poke text and graphics at once; **(C)** modern colour depth and screen size; **(G)** glyph slots filled by a TrueType engine; **(S)** sprites, colour and sound in the C64's spirit; **(B)** the Blit's bit-blit and windows; **(U)** the GPU's command queue, parallel lanes and shaders. And the other two wishes of the day: anti-tearing sugar (3.8) and the boilerplate goal (3.9).

![Layers composed at scanout](figures/gfx-layers.svg)

### Sketch 1: layered scanout, no command queue

**3.21.1** Three **layers** (a text-cell layer, a pixel layer and a sprite layer) are plain arrays in video memory that the processor pokes; the display hardware composes them as it reads them out, one pixel at a time, and there is no command queue. *(P)*

*What the programmer sees.* Three arrays, each at a fixed place, each with a small set of registers (base, size, stride, enable). The text array is the cell array of 3.11, with its glyph slots in another array; the pixel array is a framebuffer; the sprite layer is a table of entries (x, y, shape, colour, enable). A program pokes whichever it wants, in any order. *What is in video memory:* the cells, the glyph slots, the pixels, the sprite table and the sprite shapes. *At scanout:* for each pixel the hardware asks the layers from the top down (sprites, then text, then pixels), looks the cell up to its glyph, takes the glyph's coverage and the colours, and gives the first layer that is not transparent; the sprite layer needs to know only a few entries per row. The composing is done in a **line buffer** (a small memory that holds one row), as 3.15.2 already provides for. *Costs:* a compositor of the order of the 1,500 lookup tables the repository estimates for `BLT64`, plus a line buffer per layer (an estimate of mine, unmeasured); scanout traffic is the pixel layer's 498 MB/s at 1920 by 1080, 4 bytes, 60 Hz (746 MB/s at 6 bytes), plus about 13 MB/s for the cells and the glyph reads (arithmetic from the examples of 3.11). *Gives up:* anything beyond what the layers can say: no scaling or rotation, no effect that is not in the compositor; every change a program wants to make to the pixel layer is the processor writing pixels. *Properties kept:* T yes (this is the C64 and the Blit's memory-mapped screen); C yes; G yes (the text layer's glyphs); S yes (sprites, and colour per cell; sound is a separate device, 3.1.3); B no (no blitter, windows are the program's job); U no. *Text and graphics split:* healed, the C64's way: a program has text and pixels at once in one frame, with no mode switch. *A game:* sprites and a scrolling text or pixel layer, moved with a few pokes a frame; the C64 game, with a modern screen. *A terminal emulator:* pokes cells (a scroll is a few stores, or, with a base register for the layer, one store); the glyph slots are filled by the TrueType engine. *Sugar:* per-layer flip and a hardware cursor for free (3.8); the fence is the vertical-blank counter. *With virtio across the board:* needs a virtio device type of its own (reading 1 of 3.11); the layers are a device's contents, not the virtio-gpu protocol.

### Sketch 2: the same, plus a small blitter and a glyph atlas

**3.21.2** Sketch 1 plus a **blitter**, a few registers that copy and combine rectangles inside video memory (the Blit's RasterOp, the Amiga's blitter, `BLT64` of 3.10.4), and a glyph atlas that the TrueType engine fills and the text layer indexes. *(P)*

*What the programmer sees.* Sketch 1, and a handful of blitter registers: source, destination, strides, width, height, raster operation, go. The atlas is the glyph map seen as one picture (3.7): a glyph is drawn from it by the blitter into the pixel layer when a program wants a glyph on the pixel layer at an arbitrary position, and the text layer's cells point at it directly. *In video memory:* sketch 1's contents and the atlas. *At scanout:* as sketch 1. The blitter runs beside it, in the blanking time or contending for the memory. *Costs:* the blitter and its arbitration with scanout; the repository's own estimate is about 1,500 lookup tables and a line buffer, "nearly all address arithmetic" (3.10 commentary), and a bus-master port on video memory. *Gives up:* the same as sketch 1, except that drawing, scrolling and window moves in the pixel layer no longer cost the processor the pixels. *Properties kept:* T, C, G, S as sketch 1; **B yes** (bit-blit with the raster operations of 3.10.7, and windows as layers or as rectangles in the pixel layer); U no. *Split:* healed as sketch 1, and the pixel layer gains the Blit's operations, so that text on it (a window of a program that draws its own glyphs) is cheap. *A game:* sprites for the actors and the blitter for the background tiles. *A terminal emulator:* the text layer as in sketch 1; the blitter scrolls and moves windows drawn on the pixel layer. *Sugar:* as sketch 1, plus a blit that starts at the vertical blank (the "start-at-vblank" of `architecture-recommendations.md` R-9), which stops a copy from tearing. *With virtio:* as sketch 1, the blitter a feature of the device; it would be a feature bit and a queue if its commands are descriptors rather than registers (the chat chose a three-operand `draw` command, `decisions-pending.md` E3).

```
# sketch 2: scroll the pixel layer up by one text line, then draw a glyph from the atlas (placeholders)
poke(BLT_SRC, FB + LINE_H * STRIDE);  poke(BLT_DST, FB)
poke(BLT_STRIDE, STRIDE);  poke(BLT_W, W);  poke(BLT_H, H - LINE_H)
poke(BLT_OP, STORE);  poke(BLT_GO, 1);  wait_until(peek(BLT_BUSY) == 0)
```

### Sketch 3: a command-queue device with a peek/poke window

**3.21.3** A device whose main interface is a **ring of commands** in the manner of virtio-gpu, with a tiny shader-like fixed program for drawing glyphs and sprites and video memory managed by the device, and a peek/poke compatibility window on top for the programs that want to poke cells and pixels. *(P)*

![A command queue with a peek/poke window](figures/gfx-cmdqueue.svg)

*What the programmer sees.* Mostly commands: "put this text at this cell", "move this sprite", "fill this rectangle", "present"; and, as a convenience, the window: cells and pixels as plain memory that mirrors the device's. The device allocates its own video memory, so a program does not choose addresses. *In video memory:* what the device decides; the program sees handles. *At scanout:* the device renders the commands into whatever it scans out, at its own pace, and flips at the blank by itself (the sugar is free: a commit is a command). *Costs:* a command processor (a queue engine, as the virtio devices of chapter 2 already are), a drawing engine of fixed programs (the small piece of the GPU that the terminal needs: a textured quad with a glyph and two colours, a sprite), a memory manager for video memory and a consistency rule between the window and the queue; more gates than sketches 1 and 2 by a clear margin (a judgement of mine, unmeasured), and more software in the driver. *Gives up:* the plainness of sketch 1; the program must know the device's model, and two ways to reach one cell must be reconciled. *Properties kept:* T only through the window (and only as well as the window is made); C yes; G yes (the engine reads glyph slots); S yes; B no blit, but "fill" and "copy" commands do its job; **U yes**, a command queue and parallel work, but a *fixed* program, not the general shaders of 3.6. *Split:* healed by the engine, which draws text and pixels from one queue. *A game:* a command per sprite or per tile, one "present" per frame, with little processor work. *A terminal emulator:* one command per changed run of cells; the glyph atlas as in 3.7, but made by the device. *Sugar:* the fence and atomic commit are the natural form (each command completes in order; a commit is a command); versioned buffers are the device's business. *With virtio:* the natural fit, a device with queues as in 2.6, and the closest in spirit to virtio-gpu, of which it could be a profile (reading 2 of 3.11).

### Sketch 4: virtio-gpu 2D, the sugar in a library

**3.21.4** The standard virtio-gpu 2D route of 3.16, with the C64 and Blit conveniences done in software by a library (cells, glyphs, sprites and cursor, blits, flips), and no special hardware. *(P)*

*What the programmer sees.* A library that offers the peek/poke look: a cell array in memory, `put_char`, sprites as library objects, and a `present` call that does the transfer and the flush for the damaged rectangles. *In video memory:* the host's resources; on the pdp64 the program's memory is the backing (3.16.3). *At scanout:* the host reads its resource when it likes; the library cannot flip at a blank because virtio-gpu has none (3.16); tearing is the host's concern and, on an emulator, usually invisible. *Costs:* nothing in gates; the processor draws everything and a flush copies; bus traffic is the damage rectangles. The glyph slots are a library table, filled from the TrueType engine on the processor. *Gives up:* everything hardware: the hardware cursor is the cursor queue only, there are no sprites except the cursor, no blits, no layers; the C64's "the chip draws it while you do other things" is the library's loop. *Properties kept:* T only through the library (the program pokes memory the library owns, and the library presents it); C only to 32 bits (3.16.4); G yes, in software; S no (the cursor excepted); B in software; U no. *Split:* healed in the library's picture, but each screen is still one pixel framebuffer. *A game:* draws into a back buffer and flushes it, in the PC way of 3.5 (slow, but running now). *A terminal emulator:* the library draws glyphs from a cache and flushes the damaged rows. *Sugar:* damage rectangles and the fence only (3.8, 3.16). *With virtio:* it is the standard (errata 79, 95); sketches 1 to 3 would be added to it as another device or as a profile.

### Sketch 5: a c64-mode core as the terminal (a variant)

**3.21.5** A small core with the C64 family's chips, into which the host loads the terminal program by DMA (3.18), as a variant or addition to any of the sketches above: it is the terminal, and the other sketches say what the display behind it does. *(P)*

*What the programmer sees.* The host programmer sees a device that takes messages (the mailbox); the programmer of the small core sees the C64: peek, poke, a screen at a fixed place, colour RAM, glyph slots, sprites and the SID, which is the author's "as simple and pleasurable as the c64 was" at its most literal. *In video memory:* the core's screen memory, colour RAM, glyph slots, sprite shapes. *At scanout:* the VIC (or its extension) composes text, bitmap and sprites as sketch 1 does; the flip is a register of the core's own. *Costs:* a core, its memory, a DMA loader and the chips (3.18, question 7); a second program to write, load and keep secure. *Gives up:* a single, standard programming model on the host's side; the host cannot poke a cell, only send a message. *Properties kept:* T yes, for the core's programmer; C if the chips are widened as the native devices were (3.10); G yes; S yes, with sound; B only if the chip family has a blitter; U no. *Split:* healed, in the core. *A game:* a C64 game, or one written for the core. *A terminal emulator:* the core *is* the terminal; the host sends text. *Sugar:* the VIC's latches and raster interrupt, which the core's program uses; nothing is hidden from it. *With virtio:* the open tension of 3.18, question 6.

*A leaning, labelled as mine and not a recommendation of the repository.* Sketch 4 is what the first iteration already is, and the question the author has put is what the second iteration should be. Of the others, sketch 2 matches the author's words most closely (the Blit's bit-blit and the C64's text, graphics and sprites at once, no more machinery than the problem needs) and is the smallest hardware that makes peek and poke the main interface; sketch 3 is the better fit if the author wants one family with virtio-gpu and the command queue as the main interface; sketch 1 is sketch 2 without the blitter, and the blitter can be added later because it only reads and writes video memory. This leaning assumes the author wants the poke to be the main interface; if he wants commands, sketch 3 is nearer.

(3) **Do sprites exist**, as a hardware layer, as an engine primitive, or only as the cursor? (4) **Is there a blitter**, and if so as registers or as commands? (5) **Are commands or pokes the main interface**, and what does the other do? (6) **Which virtio shape**: a device type of its own, a profile of virtio-gpu, or the standard device with a library, and so what the "virtio-console" of the author's words is (3.11)? (7) **VRAM or system memory** on the first board and later? (8) **What pixel format**: 48-bit native, with the standard device at 32 bits (3.16.4)?
