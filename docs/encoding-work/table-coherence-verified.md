# Table coherence, checked against the manuals: what x86, Arm, IBM and the HATRIC paper really say, and what a guest exit really costs

*For the owner to read. Nothing here changes the manual, the grammar, the tools, the executor, the QEMU model or a test. It checks what `closing-holes-by-construction.md` said about other architectures without having read them (its section 5 listed them as "not read"), and it replaces the study's assumed cycle figures for a guest exit with published measurements. The evidence is kept in `bench/sources/` (one file for each source: full citation, address, date read, the exact sentences, a short note on what the source says; `bench/sources/README.md` is the index).*

*How to read the labels.* Every statement about another system is one of: **verified** (a sentence of the primary text, under 25 words, with its place, read on 2026-11-25; the sentence is in the source note named in brackets); **derived** (my arithmetic on verified figures, shown); **estimate** (my judgement, with the reason); or **could not verify** (what I tried is said). Where a sentence is quoted it is copied from the text; a sentence that is longer than 25 words is quoted in part and the part is marked with "...". All the PDFs were downloaded whole, converted to text and searched, not read from search-result snippets.

*Words that a PDP-11 programmer may not know.* **TLB** (translation lookaside buffer): the small cache of finished address translations, so that the processor does not walk the page tables at every access (the PDP-11/70's memory management had no such cache; every access went through its 16 relocation registers). **Page-table walk**: the hardware reading the table levels in memory to make one translation. **Walk caches** ("paging-structure caches" at Intel, "MMU caches" in the HATRIC paper): caches of the upper levels of the walk. **Stage 1 / stage 2**: a guest's own tables (guest-virtual to guest-physical) and the hypervisor's tables (guest-physical to real); Intel calls the second EPT, AMD NPT or nested page tables, Arm stage 2, RISC-V G-stage. **Hypervisor**: the program that runs guests. **Guest exit / entry** (VM exit / VM entry): the guest stops and the hypervisor runs, and back (our `GENTER` is the entry). **Hypercall**: a guest asking the hypervisor for something with a special instruction; a **null hypercall** asks for nothing, so its round trip is the pure cost of exit plus entry plus the hypervisor's minimal code. **Shootdown**: telling every other processor to drop a translation. **Serializing / barrier** (`DSB`, `ISB` at Arm; `ptesync` at Power): an instruction that makes the processor finish earlier work before going on. **Break-before-make**: Arm's rule to make an entry invalid, invalidate the TLB, and only then write the new entry. **ASID, VMID, VPID, PCID**: small numbers stored in each TLB entry to name the address space or guest it belongs to, so that a switch need not flush. **Co-tag**: HATRIC's extra tag in each TLB entry, the real address of the table entry it came from. **Directory** (cache-coherence directory): the table in a multiprocessor's cache that remembers which processors hold each memory line.

## 1. x86: what Intel and AMD guarantee

### 1.1 The caches are not coherent with stores to the tables (verified)

*What the architecture does.* The processor keeps the results of walks in the TLB and in walk caches, and does not look at those entries again against memory. The 80386 manual already says it: "operating-system programmers must flush the cache whenever the page tables are changed" (80386 manual, section 5.2.5). The current Intel manual says why: "Because the processor does not ensure that the data that it caches are always consistent with the structures in memory" (SDM vol. 3A, section 5.10, p. 5-41) software must act. AMD is blunter: "hardware snooping of TLBs during memory-reference cycles is not performed." (APM vol. 2, section 5.5.) [intel-sdm-tlb.md, amd-apm-svm-tlb.md, intel-80386-prm-tlb.md]
*What it means for us.* The pdp64's present rule (software `TLBI`) is the x86 rule. Our directory idea, or the arena, would be an addition to what x86 offers, not something that x86 already has.

### 1.2 What is guaranteed after a store, and what is not (verified)

*Not guaranteed.* "The processor may retain a TLB entry unmodified even if software subsequently modifies the relevant paging-structure entries in memory." (SDM 5.10.2.3, p. 5-43.) The same is said for each walk cache (5.10.3.1). Between the store and the invalidation "the processor may use translations based on either the old value or the new value of the paging-structure entry." (5.10.4.4, p. 5-52.) And a processor may invalidate more than it was asked to: "The processor is always free to invalidate additional entries in the TLBs and paging-structure caches." (5.10.4.1.)
*Guaranteed.* After `INVLPG`, `INVPCID`, `MOV to CR3`, `MOV to CR4` (some bits), the entries named are gone; and these are all serializing: "Note that the invalidating instructions identified in Section 5.10.4.1 are all serializing instructions." (5.10.4.3.) A page fault that comes from a stale entry removes the entry, so the fault does not repeat (5.10.4.1). `INVLPG` also clears every walk-cache entry of the current PCID.
*Not needed.* "If a paging-structure entry is modified to change the P flag from 0 to 1, no invalidation is necessary." (5.10.4.3.) The reason is that a not-present entry is never cached. Making an entry more permissive (R/W 0 to 1, XD 1 to 0) may give one "spurious" fault and nothing worse.
*What it means for us.* (i) The two cases in which the hardware is always right without help are "not present to present" and "more permissive"; the cases that need a flush are exactly "present to changed" and "less permissive". A pdp64 whose walker never caches a faulting entry gets the first for free; we should say so in the manual if it is true of our model (not checked here). (ii) The upper levels are a separate danger: Intel's walk caches may even hold an upper entry when no translation below it exists ("the processor may create a PML5E-cache entry even if there are no translations for any linear address that might use that entry"), so a stale upper entry can appear with no stale translation in the TLB. Any walk cache the pdp64 may have must be covered by whatever rule we choose (flag, directory or flush) as well as the TLB.

### 1.3 Speculative fills (verified)

*What it does.* "the processor may cache translations required for prefetches and for accesses that result from speculative execution ..." (5.10.2.3), and the multiprocessor chapter says software "must be prepared to deal with reads, instruction fetches, and prefetch requests to the affected linear-address range" from speculation (5.10.5). The read of an entry for an instruction fetch "may appear to execute before an earlier write to that paging-structure entry" unless a serializing instruction intervenes (5.10.4.3).
*What it means for us.* A rule that flushes at a point in time (flag, flush at entry) must also be ordered against walks already under way. A store that sets our flag, followed by a walker read that began before the store but fills after the flush, would put an old entry back. The design must say that the flush applies after all earlier walks have finished or been abandoned; RISC-V's manual states this very requirement for its fence (riscv-priv-sfence.md), Intel's by making the invalidations serializing.

### 1.4 INVEPT, VPID and the hypervisor's tables (verified)

*What it does.* With EPT (Intel's stage 2), the processor caches three kinds of mapping: linear (guest-virtual to real, no EPT), guest-physical (EPT alone) and combined (both stages). They are tagged by VPID (the virtual processor), PCID (the guest's address space) and the EPT root (EPTRTA). "Execution of the INVEPT instruction invalidates guest-physical mappings and combined mappings." (SDM 31.4.3.1, p. 31-23.) There are two types only: one EPT root, or all roots ("Two INVEPT types are currently defined:"): INVEPT cannot name one guest-physical page; AMD's `INVLPGA` is likewise by guest-virtual address only: "it does not provide a means to invalidate a nested translation by guest physical address." (APM 15.16.2.)
*When INVEPT is required.* "Software should use the INVEPT instruction with the “single-context” INVEPT type after making any of the following changes to an EPT paging-structure entry" (31.4.3.4, p. 31-25): a permission bit 2:0 from 1 to 0; the physical address changed; the accessed flag cleared (with accessed/dirty enabled); the large-page bit changed; memory type bits changed; the dirty flag cleared. Not needed: "it is not necessary to execute INVEPT following modification of an EPT paging-structure entry that had been not present or misconfigured." (p. 31-26.) Entries may be stale on other processors: "information from an EPT paging-structure entry may be cached on logical processors other than the one that modifies that entry."
*Entry and exit.* "VMX transitions are not required to invalidate any guest-physical mappings." (31.4.3.2, p. 31-24.) With "enable VPID" off, entries and exits invalidate the mappings of VPID 0 (31.4.3.1); with it on they need not. AMD instead lets the hypervisor ask at each entry: the control block's TLB_CONTROL field has the values "Do not flush", "Flush this guest's TLB entries", "Flush this guest's non-global TLB entries" and "Flush entire TLB" (Table 15-9), and after editing a nested page table the manual says "and intends to return to the same ASID, it should use either TLB command 011b or 001b." (APM 15.16.1.)
*What it means for us.* This is the exact analogue of our rule (a) and rule (e). Intel and AMD do **not** flush at every entry: they tag the entries so that a guest's entries survive an exit, and flush only when the hypervisor says so. AMD's own advice after editing the hypervisor's tables is to flush this guest on the next entry (a paraphrase of the sentence quoted above). Our rule (e) is that advice with the "when the hypervisor says so" replaced by "when anything stored to the tables". Our rule (a) (flush at every entry) is more than any x86 hypervisor pays, by design of the tags.

### 1.5 x86 "current" additions that look like coherence but are not

AMD has `INVLPGB` (invalidate on all processors from one instruction; the manual's sentence: "For software using INVLPGB to broadcast TLB invalidations", APM, EFER.TCE description) and Intel's Volume 4 lists registers named `RAR_CONTROL` and `RAR_ACTION_VECTOR_BASE`. Both are ways for software to send invalidations to other processors without interrupts; neither watches stores. I read no instruction text for either (Volume 3 of the APM and any Intel text on RAR were not found): what they do beyond their names is **could not verify**.

## 2. Arm: what the Arm Architecture Reference Manual says

I could not get the current issue of DDI 0487 (Arm's site builds its pages by script and gave no PDF; the files that search found were "Known issues" lists). I read two full issues from public copies: **G.b (July 2021)** and **C.a (December 2017)**. Features added after July 2021 are not covered. [arm-arm-bbm.md, linux-arm64-kvm-tlb.md]

### 2.1 The TLB is not coherent with data writes (verified)

The caches of walk results "are distinct from the data caches in that they are not required to be invalidated as the result of writes of the data." (DDI 0487G.b, D5.10, p. D5-2816.) "Whenever translation tables entries associated with a particular VMID or ASID are changed, the corresponding entries must be invalidated from the TLB" (p. D5-2817), "to ensure that these changes are visible to subsequent execution, including speculative execution".
*What it means for us.* Same as x86. Arm adds the point that the walker is "a separate observer": "a translation table walk is considered to be a separate observer", and a store to the tables "is only guaranteed to be observable after the execution of a DSB instruction by the PE that executed the store" (D5-2831). That is, even the store needs a barrier before the invalidation is worth anything. Our manual should say which of our barriers does this.

### 2.2 Break-before-make (verified)

*What it does.* "the architecture requires the use of a break-before-make sequence when changing translation table entries" whenever several processors can use the table and the change is to the memory type, cacheability, the output address (under conditions), the block size, or creates a global entry over non-global ones (D5.10.1, p. D5-2818). The steps: "Replace the old translation table entry with an invalid entry, and execute a DSB instruction.", then "Invalidate the translation table entry with a broadcast TLB invalidation instruction, and execute a DSB instruction to ensure the completion of that invalidation.", then "Write the new translation table entry, and execute a DSB instruction to ensure that the new entry is visible." The reason: so "that at no time are both the old and new entries simultaneously visible to different threads of execution".
*What it means for us.* Break-before-make is about two entries for one address living in the TLB at once (a "conflict"), which can break ordering and atomicity, not only about staleness. Our flush-at-entry and arena rules answer staleness; they do not by themselves answer "two translations at once on another processor". The study's remark that the multi-core snoop is not built (section 2.4 of the study) matters more than it said. The same rule is in Power ("software must not have two or more valid overlapping translations at any level of the translation process with different page or segment sizes") and, as a recommended algorithm, in Intel's manual (clear P, invalidate, set P, section 5.10.4.2).

### 2.3 FEAT_BBM levels (verified for G.b)

FEAT_BBM is optional and "mandatory in Armv8.4 implementations" per the feature list. It gives three levels for the narrow case of changing block size. Level 0: "Software must use break-before-make to avoid breaking coherency, ordering guarantees or uniprocessor semantics". Level 1: software may instead use a flag in the entry (the "nT" bit) that makes the entry fault while it is changed. Level 2: "Software can use the level 0 or level 1 approach and, in addition, changing block size does not break coherency", with the price that "multiple translation entries might exist within the TLB for the same input address" and give a "Conflict abort" that goes to EL2 when stage 2 is on, which `TLBI VMALLS12E1` or `TLBI ALLE1` clears.
*What it means for us.* Level 2 is the hardware promising to survive two entries for one address. It shows what hardware can really do: tolerate and report the inconsistency (an abort), not prevent it. The FEAT_BBML1 / FEAT_BBML2 names in the study come from QEMU's list; newer Arm issues may have renamed the feature, which I could not confirm from Arm's own text.

### 2.4 TLBI instructions, domains, and the barriers (verified)

The instruction names are `TLBI <type><level>{IS}`. Types: `ALL` (everything of the level), `VMALL` (all stage 1 entries of the current VMID), `VMALLS12` ("All stage 1 and stage 2 translations used at EL1 with the current VMID, if appropriate."), `ASID`, `VA`/`VAA` (by address, with or without ASID), `IPAS2` (by guest-physical address, "IPA", stage-2-only entries). `IS` at the end means "Inner Shareable": the invalidation is broadcast to every processor in the coherent group. Others (G.b) add range forms.
*Stage 2 by address needs two steps.* "It is not required to apply to caching structures that combine stage 1 and stage 2 translation table entries." (C.a, D4.10, p. D4-2208), so the manual gives the recipe, "following code is sufficient to invalidate all cached copies of the stage 2 translation of the IPA held in Xt": `TLBI IPAS2E1, Xt`; `DSB`; `TLBI VMALLE1` (D4-2216). Linux KVM on Arm does exactly this and says: "We could do so much better if we had the VA as well." and "Instead, we invalidate Stage-2 for this IPA, and the whole of Stage-1." (`arch/arm64/kvm/hyp/nvhe/tlb.c`.)
*Completion.* "A DSB ISH is sufficient to ensure completion of TLB maintenance instructions that apply to PEs in the same Inner Shareable domain." (D5-2833.) For the instruction stream on the issuing processor a `DSB` is followed by "a Context synchronization event" (an `ISB` is one): "after the execution of DSB by the PEx followed by a Context synchronization event."
*What it means for us.* Two lessons. (1) Arm has an invalidate-by-guest-physical-address instruction (x86 has not), but it only reaches stage-2-only entries and the hypervisor ends up flushing all of a guest's stage 1 anyway: so in the real world on stage-2 edits the practice is "flush everything of the guest", which is our rule (a) applied to stage-2 edits only, not to every entry. (2) Our `TLBI` family should say, as Arm's does, what is complete after which barrier; our manual may already (not checked here).

### 2.5 Generation numbers in Arm KVM (verified)

The VMID allocator, "Based on Arm64 ASID allocator algorithm", keeps a generation counter: "We're out of VMIDs, so increment the global generation count" (`arch/arm64/kvm/vmid.c`). It confirms the study's description of rule (b): the generation advances when the numbers run out, not at a reset.

## 3. IBM

### 3.1 z/Architecture (verified, SA22-7832-11, Twelfth Edition, September 2017) [ibm-poo-ipte.md]

*What it does.* "A modification of the contents of a table entry in real or absolute storage does not necessarily have an immediate effect" (chapter 3, "Translation-Lookaside Buffer"). After a change to a valid entry, "the TLB may contain both the old and the new values, and it is unpredictable whether the old or new value is selected" (p. 3-67). Entries may be made speculatively: "the CPU may form TLB entries in anticipation of future storage references or as a result of the speculative execution of instructions." Each CPU has its own TLB.
*The instructions.* `INVALIDATE PAGE TABLE ENTRY` (IPTE) sets the page-invalid bit of an entry and clears the matching entries of the TLBs: "The page-invalid bit of this page-table entry is set to one." and "this CPU signals all CPUs in the configuration to clear selected entries from their TLBs." (p. 10-39.) It waits until the other processors have finished their accesses that used the old translation. With the IPTE-range facility one execution covers 1 to 256 entries. `INVALIDATE DAT TABLE ENTRY` (IDTE) does the same for region and segment entries or for whole address spaces; `COMPARE AND SWAP AND PURGE` (CSP) purges whole TLBs: "The purging operation applies to ART-lookaside buffers (ALBs) and translation-lookaside buffers (TLBs) in all CPUs in the configuration." The model may clear more than asked, and programs must not rely on less: "the program should not take advantage of any properties obtained by a less selective clearing on a particular model" (IDTE note 1). The clearing rules also say entries "may be cleared at times other than those required".
*What the study had said and the finding.* The study recalled that `IPTE` "invalidates the translation as part of editing the entry" and called it unverified. **Verified, with one correction:** it is one instruction that edits the entry and clears every processor's TLB, but it is an instruction the program must use; a plain store to a table entry gets no such effect and falls under "unpredictable".
*What it means for us.* IBM's design is the "edit and flush are one operation" idea: it cannot be forgotten *if the kernel always uses the instruction*, but nothing stops a store. That is a weaker form of "cannot be forgotten" than the owner means (arena or directory: the plain store itself arms the flush), and a good model for our `TLBI` family if we want a single "change this entry and flush it" instruction in addition: it would not need any directory.

### 3.2 The guest under SIE (verified from the 1985 text only; modern text could not be found) [ibm-sie-guest-tlb.md]

The Principles of Operation does not describe the TLB of a guest; it says "The interpretive-execution facility is described in the publication IBM 370-XA Interpretive Execution, SA22-7095." I read SA22-7095-1 (Second Edition, September 1985, a scan). The relevant parts:
* Guest entries are tagged by the guest: "The relevant environmental information includes guest translation parameters and translation tables, host translation parameters and translation tables" (and the addresses of the guest's state description and system-control area).
* "These entries may or may not be retained when the CPU exits from the interpretive-execution mode." (They may survive the exit.)
* On every entry the hardware compares the state description's last-host-CPU field with the current processor: "If the field does not match the address, then existing guest-type TLB entries for this guest are cleared." (The guest moved: its entries here are stale.)
* "The host program may store the hex value FFFF in this field to force all guest-type TLB entries for this guest to be cleared" at the next entry. (The hypervisor's flush-at-next-entry switch.)
* A guest's own `IPTE` signals the other processors that run the same guest ("then all CPUs in the configuration are signaled to clear those guest-type TLB entries formed under a matching SCA-origin value"), through the system-control area, with an interlock bit.
*What it means for us.* IBM's guest design is: tag the entries with the guest; keep them across exits; and give the hypervisor a "flush at next entry" field (our flag!), which the hardware also sets for itself in the one case it can see (the guest changed processor). That is as close as any real design is to rule (e): the hardware takes over a part of the duty (the processor change), and the rest (table edits) stays with the hypervisor. Whether this still holds on modern machines I **could not verify**.

### 3.3 IBM POWER (verified, Power ISA 3.1C, Book III 6.10) [power-isa-tlbie.md]

"TLBs are non-coherent caches of the HTABs and Radix Trees." and "TLB entries must be invalidated explicitly with one of the TLB Invalidate instructions." (6.10.1, p. 1188.) The sequence for a table update: the stores, then `tlbie` for the same guest number (LPID), `eieio`, `tlbsync`, `ptesync`: "The most basic sequence that will achieve proper system synchronization for PTE updates is the following." And "All of the sequences require a context synchronizing operation after the sequence if the new contents of the translation table are to be used" (p. 1188). The invalidation may lag for permission downgrades: "The translation cache invalidation may lag the PTE change indefinitely and be done in bulk." Hardware may walk at any time: "Unsynchronized lookups in the Page, Segment, and when HR=0, Process Tables continue even while they are being modified."
*What it means for us.* Software duty again, guest numbers used as tags (LPID in `tlbie`). I read the update-sequence section, not the `tlbie` instruction page.

## 4. HATRIC (verified: the whole arXiv preprint, version 2 of 15 February 2017) [hatric-isca2017.md]

*Citation.* Zi Yan, Ján Veselý, Guilherme Cox, Abhishek Bhattacharjee (all at Rutgers; the owner's note said Rice), "Hardware Translation Coherence for Virtualized Systems", ISCA '17. The published version may differ from the preprint; I did not fetch it.
*What it proposes.* "we propose hardware translation invalidation and coherence or HATRIC, a readily implementable hardware mechanism to piggyback translation coherence atop existing cache coherence protocols." Each entry of the TLB, the walk caches and the nested TLB gets a co-tag: "HATRIC extends translation structure entries with coherence tags (or co-tags) storing the system physical address where the translation entry resides". The cache directory gets two bits per line, "adding two bits to record whether cache lines belong to a guest page table (gPT) or nested page table (nPT)", set by the walker: "The nPT and gPT bits are set by the hardware page table walkers on fills to the TLBs, MMU caches, and nTLBs." The hypervisor then needs nothing special: "OS and hypervisor: These can traverse, map, and unmap page table entries using standard load/store instructions."
*How it kills entries.* A store to a flagged line is already a coherence event; it is also sent to the translation structures, which drop entries with a matching co-tag. The translation structures are treated like read-only instruction caches: "Since TLBs, MMU caches, and nested TLBs are fundamentally read-only structures".
*Granularity.* The coherence unit is a cache line, eight table entries: "HATRIC conservatively invalidates all translation structure entries caching these 8 page table entries, even if only a single page table entry is modified." The co-tag is only 17 bits of the address ("Our 2 byte co-tags use bits 19-3 of the system physical address storing the page table"), so different entries can share a co-tag and extra entries are dropped; the paper says this does little harm.
*Results (all simulated).* "HATRIC achieves up to 30% performance and 10% energy benefits, for per-CPU area overheads of 2%." "HATRIC is always within 2-4% of the ideal performance." Xen: "Xen’s performance is improved by 21% and 33% for canneal and data caching respectively", on two programs. Its baseline cost: "as much as 40% of their runtime can be wasted on translation coherence" (only under page remapping between two memory speeds).
*Hardware cost.* The only cost stated is the "2%" per-core area, attributed to "detailed RTL modeling" without a table in the preprint; a full 8-byte co-tag would double a TLB entry, which is why the co-tag is cut to 2 bytes. It needs a directory-based cache-coherence protocol (the paper also made it work on MOESI and on snooping protocols such as MESIF) and the two directory bits.
*What it assumes.* "Our discussion focuses on x86-64 systems."; a simulator ("We use a trace-based approach to drive our simulation framework.") of a 32-CPU Haswell-like machine, driven by traces from KVM on a two-socket Xeon in which a fast and a slow memory were imitated by memory contention. The workload is a hypervisor moving pages between a fast and a slow memory (the pages being remapped are data pages; the paper says "less than 1% of page remappings" are of table pages), not guest resets or hypercalls.
*Does a shipping processor do it?* The paper does not say so. I searched the web and the Intel, AMD, Arm, IBM and RISC-V manuals and found none (a negative of this sort is **could not verify**; report it as "none found").
*How it differs from the study's rule (c).* The study's directory of walked table pages, a content-addressed store checked on every store (6,030 to 28,757 gates), is what HATRIC calls UNITD, its predecessor, and rejects: "HATRIC uses energy-frugal co-tags instead of UNITD’s large reverse-lookup CAM circuitry" (section 4.4). The study's other variant, "a bit in each data-cache line that the walker read, plus the table page's address in each buffer entry" (estimated at 8,000 to 11,000 gates), **is** HATRIC. The paper gives 2% of a core's area for it. On the pdp64's core (220,000 to 310,000 gates) that is 4,400 to 6,200 gates if area followed gates, which it does not exactly (the area of memory arrays and of a directory is not the same as that of logic); I read the paper's 2% as the same order as the study's 8,000 to 11,000, not as a confirmation of it.
*What it means for us.* (i) HATRIC is the one design in the literature that is the owner's idea. (ii) It needs a coherent, directory-based cache hierarchy, so it is a multi-core design; for a single-core first version the arena (two comparators on the core's own store port) is simpler. (iii) It keeps entries precise and avoids the interrupts and exits of a shootdown, which is a multiprocessor benefit; our study counted the cost of a flush at entry in a single guest.

## 5. Does any shipping architecture keep the TLB coherent with page-table stores in hardware?

I found none. What each says (all read 2026-11-25; details in the source notes):

| architecture | what the manual says | verified how |
|---|---|---|
| x86, old (80386, 1986) | "operating-system programmers must flush the cache whenever the page tables are changed." | 80386 manual 5.2.5 |
| x86, current (Intel, AMD) | no coherence; software invalidates; AMD: "hardware snooping of TLBs ... is not performed". `INVLPGB` and Intel's RAR are software-started broadcasts. | SDM 325462-093; APM 3.46 |
| Arm | "not required to be invalidated as the result of writes of the data"; `TLBI` + `DSB` + `ISB`; break-before-make. | DDI 0487 G.b, C.a |
| RISC-V | `SFENCE.VMA` and `HFENCE.GVMA` "synchronize page table writes and the address translation hardware"; the fence orders "only the local hart’s" references; `Svinval` splits the fence for speed; `Svadu` lets hardware update accessed and dirty bits. The H-extension manual says tagging entries with the guest-physical address "is costly", so the common technique is to flush all entries of the guest. | Privileged ISA sources, main branch |
| IBM z | `IPTE` and `IDTE` edit and clear the TLBs of all CPUs in one instruction; a plain store: "unpredictable". | PoO SA22-7832-11 |
| IBM POWER | "TLBs are non-coherent caches"; the `tlbie` / `eieio` / `tlbsync` / `ptesync` sequence. | Power ISA 3.1C |
| Itanium | "The processor does not ensure the TLBs are coherent with the VHPT memory image."; purges by `ptc.g`/`ptc.ga` reach "at least the processors on the same local bus", and "Software must handle the case where a purge does not propagate to all processors". | SDM vol. 2 rev. 2.1 (2002) |
| SPARC V9 | the (informative) MMU appendix only requires "a mechanism for privileged software to invalidate" translations. Implementation manuals not read. | SPARC V9, F.7 |
| MIPS | the TLB is written by software; an optional hardware walker accelerates the refill ("Hardware acceleration for page table walking is an optional feature in the architecture."); no store-coherence rule found in the volume. | MIPS32 PRA rev. 6.02 (2015) |
| Alpha | "Whenever a non-software field of a valid page table entry (PTE) is modified, copies of that PTE must be made coherent."; the means are PALcode invalidations. | Alpha ARM 4th ed. (2002) |

*What it means for us.* The hardware that exists helps in four ways only: it lets software invalidate more precisely (by address, address space, guest, guest-physical address, range); it lets software invalidate everywhere with one instruction (Arm `...IS`, AMD `INVLPGB`, IBM `IPTE`); it lets entries be tagged so that a switch need not flush (ASID, VMID, VPID, PCID, EPTRTA, the SIE guest tags); and it lets the hypervisor ask for a flush at the next entry (AMD `TLB_CONTROL`, the SIE last-host field). All of it is started by software. The hardware that notices the store itself exists only in the research of the section above (HATRIC and its predecessor UNITD, 2010, named in that paper).

### 6.2 What these say about the study's assumption

The study took 3 cycles for a table read and 20 cycles each for an exit and an entry, so a null hypercall at about 48 cycles. The figures say:
* The hardware part alone is not 40 cycles on x86: it is 761 to 784 cycles on the processors measured, because VMX saves and loads the guest's whole state.
* The whole round trip (hardware plus the hypervisor's minimal code) is 376 (Arm, hypervisor in its own level, only the general registers saved), 546 to 739 (RISC-V, FPGA), 1,228 to 1,300 (x86, 2015), and in 2024 server software about 2,300 to 7,100 cycles (estimate). That is **8 times (376) to 27 times (1,300) the study's 48 cycles**, and more for a full KVM.
* For the Arm figure the paper's own breakdown shows where it goes: of the same machine's KVM figures, saving and restoring the general registers costs 152 + 184 = 336 cycles. A guest exit on the pdp64 with a software save and restore of its 32 registers will be of this order, whatever the hardware does. If I take 376 minus 336, the processor's own trap and return is about 40 cycles (**derived**; the paper does not say so). So the study's "20 + 20" is about right **for the trap alone, if the hardware does as little as Arm's EL2 trap**, and misses everything the software must do.
* Whether the pdp64's `GENTER` and exit are as cheap as Arm's EL2 trap, or as dear as VMX, depends on how much state they switch: not known; that is a design choice (the study's mask idea for the registers, section 3 of the study, would cut the software part).

### 6.3 The read cost

The study charged 3 cycles for a table read (the repository's timing model, an **estimate** it admitted). I have no primary source for real read latencies on a walk (a cache miss costs many times more). To show the effect I use 10 and 30 cycles as two **assumptions of mine**, not measured values, next to the study's 3.

### 6.4 Recomputing the study's cost of rule (a), the flush at every guest entry

Per entry of a small guest the study measured 16 extra table reads (`hv-first`). Added to a null hypercall round trip T, the flush costs (**derived**: 16 x r / T):

| cost of a read r | T = 48 (study) | T = 376 (Arm EL2) | T = 1,300 (x86 2015) | T = 3,000 (a 2024 KVM, estimate) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 cycles (study), 48 per entry | +100% | +13% | +3.7% | +1.6% |
| 10 cycles (assumed), 160 | +333% | +43% | +12% | +5.3% |
| 30 cycles (assumed), 480 | +1,000% | +128% | +37% | +16% |

Over the whole of the study's measured programs (extra reads x r, over entries x T): `hv-first`, 1,266 extra reads in 79 entries, becomes +100% (study), +13% (T = 376), +3.7% (T = 1,300) at r = 3; +43% and +12% at r = 10; +128% and +37% at r = 30. `hv-stage2`, 51 extra reads in 38 entries, becomes +8.4% (study), +1.1% (T = 376), +0.3% (T = 1,300) at r = 3, and up to +11% (T = 376) and +3.1% (T = 1,300) at r = 30.
The study's worst case, a guest that has to refill all 48 entries of both buffers (48 x 15 reads), is a bound that does not depend on T: 2,160 cycles at r = 3 (the study's figure), 7,200 at r = 10, 21,600 at r = 30. At a 1 ms timer tick on a 1 GHz core that is 0.2%, 0.7% or 2.2%. At 100,000 exits a second it is 22%, 72% or beyond the whole core, but the exits alone, at the real costs, already take 3.8% (T = 376), 13% (T = 1,300) or 30% (T = 3,000) of that core, so 100,000 exits a second is not a realistic load for the larger figures.

### 6.5 What it does to the study's conclusions

1. **"Rule (a) about doubles a hypercall that changed nothing" holds only under the 20-cycle assumption.** With the published round trips, it is a few percent (x86) to roughly an eighth (Arm-like EL2, with 3-cycle reads) of a hypercall, and to two-fifths if reads cost 10. It stays large only if the exit is as cheap as the study assumed and the reads are slow, or in the worst case of a full refill. So the study's decision 4 ("discarding at every `GENTER` is acceptable for a first version") is **more** acceptable than it said, and its decision 1 recommendation of rule (e) rests less on speed than the study claimed.
2. **Rule (e)'s speed case is weaker, its safety case is unchanged.** The arena saves, per entry of a guest that changed nothing, 16 x r cycles, 48 to 480, against exits that cost hundreds to thousands; and it costs about 1,300 gates (0.5% of the core). The case for it is now that "the hypervisor cannot forget", not that it saves a doubling. The study's text for decision 2 (the 6,000 to 28,000 gate directory against the 1,300 gate arena) is not changed by cycles: gates are gates.
3. **The directory and the cache-line variant are not made better or worse by the cycle figures**, except that their saving, like the arena's, is smaller against a dearer exit. HATRIC's reported gain is for a different workload (a hypervisor remapping pages continuously, where flushes and shootdown interrupts, 640 to 1,300 cycles each, dominate), not for guest entries.
4. **The study's cycle figures for the exit and entry should not be quoted in the manual**; if a number is needed there, give the hardware-alone figure only for a design that has measured it, and say which software saves the registers.
I made no change to the study; this section is the correction, for the owner to decide whether to fold in.

## 7. The conclusion in plain words

*What the others guarantee.* Nothing, about stores to the tables. Every architecture read says the cache of translations may keep old entries after a store, that the processor may use the old or the new or both, and that it may even have made the entries before the program asked, by guessing. What they guarantee is the effect of their own invalidating instructions, with their barriers, and that a not-present entry is never cached (so making an entry present needs nothing).
*What they require.* Software must invalidate after every change that makes an entry less permissive or different, with the right barriers; for some changes (Arm, Power) it must break before making, because two entries for one address at once is a hazard of its own; on several processors it must reach the others too (shootdown, or a broadcast instruction).
*What they offer to make that cheap.* Tags (ASID, VMID, VPID, PCID, the guest number in `tlbie`, the guest tags of SIE), so that entries survive a switch of guest; invalidation by address, by guest, by guest-physical address (Arm only), by range; one instruction that reaches every processor (Arm `IS`, AMD `INVLPGB`, IBM `IPTE`); and, for the hypervisor, a request to flush at the next entry (AMD `TLB_CONTROL`, SIE's last-host field) which the hypervisor may forget.
*What that says about the owner's idea.* Hardware that cannot be forgotten, because the store itself arms the flush, is **not** in any of these architectures: IBM comes nearest (the edit and the flush are one instruction, but a plain store is still unsafe), and the SIE field is the nearest to our flag (hardware sets it for itself when the guest moves processor). The only design that does what the owner wants for every store is HATRIC: simulated, x86 only, and paid for with a directory and a co-tag in every entry. So the pdp64 would be the first shipped design to have it, which has two sides: a real distinction, and no field experience (every shipped design has been shaken out by thousands of operating systems; ours would not be).
*The cheap form is sound.* Rule (e), the arena and the "tables changed" flag, is what AMD tells hypervisors to do by hand after editing nested tables (to flush this guest on the next entry), with the "when the hypervisor says so" replaced by "when anything stored into the arena", and it costs two comparators. Everything the manuals say for single-processor use is covered by it **if** four things hold, each of which the manuals make a requirement and the study should state: (i) the walker never caches a faulting entry (so that invalid-to-valid needs no flush); (ii) any walk cache is flushed by the same flag; (iii) a flush applies after all earlier walks have finished or been abandoned (the speculative fill, 1.3); (iv) with more than one core, the store of another core must reach the flag, and break-before-make's two-entries hazard must be covered by a rule between cores (the study left this unbuilt).
*Cycles.* The real exit costs hundreds to thousands of cycles, not 40; so a flush at every entry is a few percent to some tens of percent for a small guest, not a doubling, and a default of "flush at entry" is acceptable for a first version, as the study allowed, though it is more than any shipped hypervisor pays (they tag).
