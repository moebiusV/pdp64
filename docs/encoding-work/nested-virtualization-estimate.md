# What it would cost to let a guest run its own guests (nested virtualization): an estimate

*For the owner to read. Nothing here changes the manual, the grammar, the tools, the executor, the QEMU model, a test or a record. The owner's words (the form of 2026-11-19, on the decision "the hypervisor level has its own translation roots and tag" and the rule "no nested virtualization in version 1", manual 6.14.7): "i'm ok with not allowing nested guests, i'd need a real estimate of how much extra gates and work it would be to allow nested guests." This is that estimate.*

*How to read the labels.* Every figure is one of three kinds, and says which. **Measured**: I ran a program or counted lines in the repository (the program or file is named). **Counted from the repository**: read from a manual statement, an errata number or code (file:line). **Estimate**: my judgement, with the reasoning and a range. A statement about another architecture is either **verified** (a short quotation, under 25 words, with its address and the date I read it, 2026-11-19) or marked **unverified** and kept out of the conclusions. No cycle count of a pdp64 exit exists anywhere in the repository, so no time is claimed for one. Person-days are for a human engineer and are estimates; section 6 says what the repository's own record shows about how the base work was actually done.*

## 0. The answer in one page

* **Software nesting only (design point A): zero new flip-flops and zero new gates are needed for the guest hypervisor to be *safe*; a guest can never escape the real hypervisor.** I checked 12 hardware points (section 4.2); the accepted design already provides ten. Two things are missing for a guest hypervisor to be *correct* (to run unmodified and work): the instructions that mean one thing at `H` = 1 and another at `H` = 0 (`RTI`, `RTT`, `PUSHX`, `POPX`, taking an exception, reading `MMUCTL`) cannot be trapped today, and the memory type of the three-way composition is not defined. The one small amendment is **one trap-control bit, `VH`, in the reserved bit 15 of `HTRAP`**: **measured 14 NAND2 equivalents and 1 flip-flop** for its decision (about 20 in all), **estimated 70 to 220** with the routing, that is **0.02% to 0.1% of the scalar core** (220,000 to 310,000, `docs/encoding-work/vec-gates.md`).
* **Hardware-assisted nesting (design point B)** has two independent pieces. **B1, an Arm-NEVE-like page that takes the guest hypervisor's register moves without an exit:** one 28-bit page register and an enable (29 flip-flops, 174 NAND2 equivalents), a decode that **measured 13 NAND2 more** than the accepted design's decision, and an estimated 500 to 2,000 for the control of a physical load or store from a register move: **about 700 to 2,200 in all, 0.2% to 1.0% of the core**. **B2, a hardware walk through two second stages** (nobody has built it; see section 3): a second root register (29 flip-flops), a second tag in the translation buffer (**measured +8,322 NAND2-equivalents for a 32-entry buffer, so an estimated +12,500 for the core's two buffers**) and walker control (estimated 3,000 to 10,000): **about 16,000 to 23,000, 5% to 10% of the core**, and the cold walk grows from 15 to 63 table reads (computed).
* **Work (estimates, a human engineer):** point A: **about 45 to 105 person-days** (9 to 21 person-weeks), of which the real hypervisor's nesting software is 20 to 60. B1 adds **15 to 30** and *removes* most of the exit multiplication. B2 adds **30 to 60** and I do not recommend it.
* **Cost at run time, from other architectures' measurements (not the pdp64's):** without hardware help one exit of the guest's guest turns into many exits of the guest hypervisor: 13 or 31 on x86 (Turtles), 82 or 126 on Arm v8.3, and for the pdp64 I count **about 4 exits for a minimal hypervisor and about 25 to 30 for a full world switch** (section 5, counted from `docs/encoding-work/hypervisor-design.md` 1.3 and 2.7).
* **Recommendation:** keep 6.14.7 for version 1, do **not** build nesting, and do not build any hardware assist. Record, for free, that the door is open: (1) the accepted design already makes "no nesting" a software policy of the real hypervisor, not a hardware limit (the hardware always exits; the real hypervisor decides what to do with the exit); (2) reserve `HTRAP` bit 15 and one exit code for `VH` by a sentence; (3) settle the memory-type composition (amendment A3 of `best-of-breed-mmu-virt.md`), which is needed with or without nesting. Every one of the three other architectures added hardware assist **after** software nesting existed and had been measured (section 2); the same order costs the pdp64 least.

## 1. What nesting means here, in plain language

Today (6.14, errata 195, 196) a machine runs three kinds of software. A **guest** is a whole operating system that thinks it owns the machine; the **hypervisor** is the small kernel underneath that runs the guests; the machine's hardware has a mode bit, `H`, that is 1 only while the hypervisor runs (`hypervisor-design.md` 1.1). When a guest does something that belongs to the hypervisor (writes a page-table register, executes `HALT`, is interrupted by a device) the hardware **exits**: it stops the guest and starts the hypervisor, which looks at the reason and then **enters** a guest again with the instruction `GENTER`.

**Nested virtualization** is running a hypervisor *as* a guest. Give the levels names, as the other architectures do:

* **L0**: the real hypervisor, the only software that runs at `H` = 1.
* **L1**: a guest that is itself a hypervisor. It believes it is at `H` = 1; really it runs at `H` = 0 like any guest.
* **L2**: a guest of L1. It runs at `H` = 0 too; L1 thinks it created it.

Nothing is special to the hardware about L1 and L2: both are guests of L0. What makes nesting hard is that **L0 must make L1's belief true**: when L1 writes a hypervisor register, or executes `GENTER`, or edits its own second-stage page tables, L0 must pretend that the real hardware did it, and when L2 stops, L0 must decide whether the stop is L0's business or L1's, and if L1's, make it look to L1 like an exit from its own hardware.

The pdp64's accepted rule is the simplest possible: 6.14.7, "There is no nested virtualization in version 1: a guest's use of any hypervisor control traps to the hypervisor" (`docs/manual-src/06-kernel.md:576`). The code does exactly that: a guest's `MFSR` or `MTSR` of a number from 128 to 147 raises exit code 18 (`common/pdp64exec.c:655-663`, the test at line 661), and a guest's `GENTER` does the same (`common/pdp64exec.c:429`). The first hypervisor's outline says what L0 then does: "18 (a nesting attempt: inject the illegal-instruction exception into the guest)" (`hypervisor-design.md` 6.6). So **today nesting is forbidden by L0's policy, not by a missing mechanism.** This document asks what it costs to change the policy to "emulate".

## 2. The baseline: what the other architectures do

All three do the same thing first: **trap and emulate**. L1 runs deprivileged; every hypervisor instruction it executes traps to L0; L0 keeps a virtual copy of the hypervisor state and acts on L1's behalf. They then diverge on how much hardware help was added afterwards.

### 2.1 x86 (Intel VMX)

* **Software nesting exists and runs unmodified hypervisors.** Quote (Linux kernel documentation, "Nested VMX", https://docs.kernel.org/virt/kvm/x86/nested-vmx.html, read 2026-11-19): "correctly and efficiently emulating them using the single level of VMX available in the hardware." The same page names the paper below as its theory.
* **The origin: the Turtles project** (Ben-Yehuda et al., "The Turtles Project: Design and Implementation of Nested Virtualization", OSDI 2010, https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/osdi10/tech/full_papers/Ben-Yehuda.pdf, read 2026-11-19). Quote: "Despite the lack of architectural support for nested virtualization in the x86 architecture". Their measured overhead of one nested level: kernbench 14.5%, SPECjbb 7.82%, falling to 10.3% and 6.3% if L1's `vmread` and `vmwrite` did not trap (section 4.1 of the paper). "within 6-8% of single-level" is their abstract's claim for common workloads with their I/O and paging optimizations.
* **Exit multiplication** (the term is the paper's): "when L1 handles a PIO exit of L2, it generates on average 31 additional exits, whereas in the cpuid case ... only 13 exits are required" (section 4.1.1, as I read it; the quotation is shortened, the numbers are theirs). With `vmread`/`vmwrite` discounted the numbers fall to 14 and 2. A mode switch pair cost "approximately 1,000" cycles on their machine (section 4.3).
* **The second stage (nested EPT).** There is **no hardware nested second stage in the sources I read**; L0 builds a *shadow* table, called EPT0→2 in the paper, that composes L1's table (EPT1→2) with L0's own for L1 (EPT0→1). How L0 learns that L1 changed its table: quote (same paper, section 3.3): "L0 sets the memory area of EPT1→2 as read-only, thereby causing a trap when L1 tries to update it." L0 also traps L1's `INVEPT`. The paper reports "more than a three-fold speedup" from this multi-dimensional paging against shadowing a shadow (section 4.1.2).
* **The later hardware assist: VMCS shadowing.** Verified only through the Arm paper of 2.2, which says (read 2026-11-19): "VMCS shadowing redirects instructions that are designed to access the VMCS" and measured "roughly a 10% performance improvement". I did not read Intel's own text for it (unverified from a primary Intel source).
* **Linux KVM's shadow-table rule** (https://docs.kernel.org/virt/kvm/x86/mmu.html, read 2026-11-19): for the nested case it lists the translations "(ngva->)ngpa->gpa->hpa" encoded by shadow entries, and it says of the guest's own page tables "the guest is obliged to issue an invlpg instruction before using the translation", which is why KVM may leave some of them unprotected and synchronise at the invalidate. This is the classic idea the pdp64 can reuse (4.2, the detail of points 10 and 6).

### 2.2 Arm (FEAT_NV, FEAT_NV2)

I could not fetch the Arm Architecture Reference Manual (its pages are built by script; the previous document, `best-of-breed-mmu-virt.md` section 6, had the same result). The sources are the paper whose last author works at Arm, which describes the architecture it measures, and QEMU.

* **FEAT_NV (Armv8.3): trap and emulate by architecture.** Paper (Lim, Dall, Li, Nieh, Zyngier, "NEVE: Nested Virtualization Extensions for ARM", SOSP 2017, https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~nieh/pubs/sosp2017_neve.pdf, read 2026-11-19). It lists four kinds of instruction that a guest hypervisor deprivileged to the guest level must have trapped: those that touch hypervisor-level registers; those that touch the guest-level registers the hypervisor uses for its VM (they "will now interfere with the execution of the guest hypervisor which is really running in EL1"); `ERET` and reading the current level ("the eret instruction is paravirtualized to trap to EL2 and reading the CurrentEL special register is paravirtualized to return EL2"); and the VHE instructions. **This is the same set of problem instructions as the pdp64's, found independently** (section 4.2, point 11).
* **The measured cost without more hardware.** Quote: "it causes 126 and 82 traps to the host hypervisor when running in a nested VM" for a hypercall that causes one trap in a single-level VM (a hypervisor without and with the host-extension, VHE). Memcached "goes from more than a 40 times slowdown using ARMv8.3 to less than a 3 times slowdown using NEVE".
* **FEAT_NV2, the NEVE proposal (included in Armv8.4 according to the paper).** One register, `VNCR_EL2`, holds the physical base of a page; "redirect system register access instructions from VM registers to memory at a specified offset". The guest hypervisor's reads and writes of its hypervisor registers become plain loads and stores of that page; the host reads the page when it needs the values. The paper says the architecture change is "relatively small": the register plus redirect logic. **QEMU has both** (`docs/system/arm/emulation.rst` lists FEAT_NV and FEAT_NV2; the register is `VNCR_EL2` at `target/arm/helper.c:5794-5813`; each redirected register carries a page offset, `nv2_redirect_offset`, in `target/arm/helper.c`; the trap bits are `HCR_NV` and `HCR_NV1` in `target/arm/cpu.h`). My count of the QEMU Arm sources: 14 files mention the nesting features and about 113 lines match (a rough grep, an under-count).
* **The second stage: no hardware nesting.** Quote: "the host hypervisor creates shadow Stage-2 page tables to map from L2 VM PAs to L0 PAs". Three translations are needed and the hardware has two.

### 2.3 RISC-V (the H extension)

* **The specification invites software nesting and defers hardware.** Privileged specification v20260120, hypervisor chapter (https://docs.riscv.org/reference/isa/v20260120/priv/hypervisor.html, read 2026-11-19): "Hypervisors can support nested virtualization analogously." (said of running the extension on a platform that lacks it, by trapping to machine mode). It also says the machine level must be able to merge translation stages "to improve support for nested hypervisors, i.e., running hypervisors atop other hypervisors", which is a statement of a *future* need, not a feature.
* **Trap bits for a guest hypervisor exist:** `hstatus.VTSR` makes the guest's `SRET` raise a virtual-instruction exception ("When VTSR=1, an attempt in VS-mode to execute SRET raises a virtual-instruction exception"); `VTVM` does the same for `SFENCE.VMA` and `satp`. A guest's use of `HFENCE` and the hypervisor CSRs also raises it. That is the RISC-V counterpart of the Arm trap bits and of the `VH` bit proposed below.
* **NACL, the SBI nested acceleration extension (a software interface, not an instruction).** SBI specification v3.0, chapter 15 (https://docs.riscv.org/reference/sbi/v3.0/ext-nested-acceleration.html, read 2026-11-19): it "allows both to collaboratively reduce traps taken by the L0 hypervisor for emulating RISC-V H-extension functionality". The L1 hypervisor registers a shared page (4096 bytes of scratch space and an array of 1024 words, one for each H-extension CSR); it writes its CSR and `HFENCE` requests there and the L0 hypervisor emulates them at an explicit synchronisation call. It is **Arm's NEVE idea done in software, by agreement between L1 and L0**, so no silicon is needed. The group's page for ratification and the chapter's date I did not check beyond the text read (the search result said "ratified as part of SBI v2.0", from a mailing-list patch: unverified).
* **No hardware nested second stage** appears in the specification text I read.

### 2.4 What the software cost is, in all three

* **Shadow second stage.** L0 keeps a table that composes L1's second stage with its own, fills it lazily on faults, and discards it on L1's invalidates. Cost: one exit of L0 for the first touch of each page of L2, plus a software walk of L1's table.
* **Exit multiplication.** Every hypervisor instruction in L1's exit handler is an exit to L0 (the numbers above). The assists (VMCS shadowing, NV2, NACL) attack exactly this, by turning register moves into memory moves.

**The pattern.** x86: software nesting 2010, hardware help (VMCS shadowing) later. Arm: trap and emulate in Armv8.3, redirection to memory in Armv8.4 after a measurement showed 126 traps. RISC-V: software nesting from the first specification, a *software* assist (NACL) afterwards. No one built a hardware walk through two second stages in the sources I read (the claim "none exists" is **unverified for processors newer than the sources**).

## 3. The pdp64's baseline for this question

* **State bit and register block.** `H`, `HVE`, registers 128 to 147 (`hypervisor-design.md` 1.1, 1.3; errata 195 decisions 1 to 6). `HTRAP` has bits 0 to 14 and 16 to 23 assigned; **bit 15 and bits 24 to 63 are reserved**, and a write of a one to a reserved bit is an illegal instruction "so software can probe for a bit that a later version defines" (`hypervisor-design.md` 1.5; the code, `common/pdp64exec.c:471`).
* **Exits carry a syndrome**: `HEXITR` (code 18 for a guest's hypervisor register or `GENTER`), `HEPC`, `HEPSW`, `HEVA`, `HGPA`, `HINSN` (the first 32 bits of the instruction), `HCAUSE` (1.3, 1.6; code `hexit`, `common/pdp64exec.c:352`).
* **Stage 2** is the page-table entry of 6.7 with `U` and `G` zero, three levels, a two-page root, accessed and dirty bits in software (a clear one is an exit), one 16-bit tag in `GTBR` (1.7; code `s2_walk`, `qemu/target-pdp64/mmu.c:67`). The architectural cache of the QEMU model is keyed by (tag, `ASID`, mode index, page) (errata 196).
* **Invalidation rules G-a to G-d** (1.7): a guest's own invalidates act on its tag; at `H` = 1 `TLBIA` discards the tag in `GTBR`; "A change to a stage-2 entry that was valid is followed by `TLBIA` for the guest and `TLBSYNC`."
* **Cost of the base, for scale.** The scalar core is 220,000 to 310,000 NAND2 equivalents, of which translation is 34,000 to 38,000 (`vec-gates.md`, the scalar-core table). The guest tag is *not* in that figure. **Measured by me with the repository's method** (`tools/gates` flow: yosys, `synth -flatten`, `abc -g NAND`, flip-flops at 6; a 32-entry buffer written as in `tools/gates/core.v` `tlb32`, which I reproduced at 7,588 logic and 2,048 flip-flops against the record's 7,589): adding a valid bit, one 16-bit tag in each entry, the match and an invalidate by tag costs **+5,564 logic and +544 flip-flops, +8,828 NAND2 equivalents for a 32-entry buffer**. For the core's two buffers (16 and 32 entries, the record's factor of 1.5) that is an **estimated +13,000** for the accepted design's guest tag, which was never counted before. It is the yardstick for the nested tag below.

## 4. Design point A: software nesting only

### 4.1 What L0 must do (the list)

L0 keeps, in memory, a **virtual copy of the hypervisor state of L1**: the 20 registers of 1.3, L1's `H` bank of `R14` and `DP`, and a flag "L1 is at virtual `H` = 1" (running its hypervisor code) or "L1 is running L2". Then:

1. **Emulate L1's moves of hypervisor registers.** Each `MFSR` or `MTSR` of 128 to 147 by L1 exits (code 18); L0 decodes the register number and the general register from `HINSN` and reads or writes the virtual copy, then resumes after the instruction (a trap: `HEPC` is the next instruction). 20 registers, read and write, with the five read-only ones (`HEXITR`, `HEVA`, `HCAUSE`, `HGPA`, `HINSN`) refusing a write.
2. **Emulate L1's `GENTER`** (exit code 18 too). L0 loads L2's registers from the virtual copy (the status word and PC from virtual `HEPSW` and `HEPC`; banks; `VCPUID`; `VIRQ`, `VIVEC`; `VTOFF`), computes L2's real `HTRAP`, `GTBR` (the shadow, below) and `VTOFF`, then does a real `GENTER` of L2.
3. **Compose the second stages.** L1's `GTBR` names L1's table (a pointer in L1-guest-physical space). L0 builds a *shadow* stage 2 in host memory, in the same format (the hardware's, so no translation of formats), with the tag of L2: leaf = L2-guest-physical page → host page by walking L1's table in software, then L0's own table for L1; **permissions are the intersection, the size the smaller of the two leaves, the accessed and dirty bits set only if L1's entry has them set** (so an L2 access that L1 wanted to see as a stage-2 fault is a fault in the shadow too). Fill on faults: a fault in the shadow is an exit to L0 with `HGPA` and the flags `WALK` and `VEC` (1.6); L0 walks L1's table; if L1's own entry is invalid or refuses the access, the fault is **L1's**: L0 forwards it (point 5); otherwise L0 fills the shadow entry and enters L2 again. A guest page-table fault of L2's *stage 1* is L2's own and is delivered to L2 by the hardware with no exit.
4. **Invalidate.** L1's `TLBI`, `TLBIA`, `TLBSYNC` at virtual `H` = 1 must trap (the `HTRAP` bits 4 to 6 exist); L0 discards the matching shadow entries and does the real `TLBIA` with the shadow's tag (G-d), with the broadcast of 6.8.3.
5. **Forward exits of L2 to L1.** An exit of L2 reaches L0 (with L0's `HTRAP` = the **union** of the bits L0 needs and the bits L1 set for L2, and the hypercall mask `TRAPMASK` likewise). If L1 asked for the event, L0 writes L1's virtual `HEXITR`, `HEPC`, `HEPSW`, `HEVA`, `HCAUSE`, `HGPA`, `HINSN`, switches the `H` banks in software, sets the flag "L1 at virtual `H` = 1" and resumes L1 at its `HVBR` + 8 * `HCAUSE`. If L1 did not ask for it, L0 handles it (for example a `WFE` that L0 needs to trap but L1 left alone) and enters L2 again.
6. **Virtual interrupts.** L1's `VIRQ` and `VIVEC` for L2 go to the real registers, **merged** with anything L0 itself injects. The delivery to L2 is by hardware with no exit (1.8), which is a gain over x86 and Arm in this respect (section 5).
7. **Timer.** L2's offset is the sum of L0's offset for L1 and L1's offset for L2 (modulo 2^64): L0 writes the real `VTOFF`. The virtual timer page's compare and enable registers are shared hardware: L0 saves and restores them at every switch between L1's and L2's timers (the table of 2.7 already lists them as world-switch state).
8. **Physical interrupts** always exit to L0 (6.14.5); L0 forwards to L1 the ones L1 owns, as exits.
9. **Reset of L2** is an operation L1 performs (2.6), made of L1's register writes, so it works by points 1 and 2 if the tag rules of point 4 are followed.
10. **IOMMU for an L2 device** (outside what the owner asked, but part of "nested guests"): the IOMMU's level B belongs to L0; L1's virtual unit (6.14.6, already trapped) would have to be composed with L0's level B for L2's device the way point 3 composes stage 2. Not examined further; an estimate is in section 6.

### 4.2 What the architecture must guarantee, and whether it does: the twelve hardware points I checked

Safety first (the repository's own principle P1, `best-of-breed-mmu-virt.md` section 0): **can a hostile L1 or L2 harm L0 or another guest?** For each point: *provided* = already in the accepted design and code; *missing* = needs a decision.

| # | Hardware point | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Every use of a hypervisor register (numbers 128 to 147) and of `GENTER` by a guest exits, after the guest's own privilege check | **provided**: `common/pdp64exec.c:429`, `:655-663`; test `tests/sim/hv-exits.S` |
| 2 | The exit says *which* instruction: `HINSN` holds the first 32 bits; `MFSR` and `MTSR` are two parcels | **provided**: `hypervisor-design.md` 1.4, 3.1; the model fills `HINSN` from `cur_insn` (`common/pdp64exec.c:352`, `:377-381`) |
| 3 | An unassigned system-register number is illegal *in the guest*, so L1 cannot invent registers | **provided**: errata 195 decision 4, audit 12(c) |
| 4 | A stage-2 fault gives the guest-physical address, the access kind, the guest-virtual address, whether it was a table read or a vector fetch | **provided**: `HGPA`, `HCAUSE` 18 to 20, `HEVA`, flags `WALK`, `VEC` (1.6) |
| 5 | "Which stage failed": there is one stage 2; a stage-1 fault is never an exit but the guest's own | **provided**: by construction (2.5 of the design); no extra syndrome is needed because L0 decides ownership by its software walk of L1's table |
| 6 | Invalidate by guest tag, and the invalidates of a guest act only on its own tag | **provided**: rules G-a to G-d (1.7), tests `tests/qemu/hv-stage2.S` (138 checks) |
| 7 | L1's `TLBI`, `TLBIA`, `TLBSYNC`, `MTASID`, writes of `PTBR`, `MMUCTL`, `VBR` and `MFCPUID`, `SPL`/`MTPS`, `WFE`, `SEV` can all be made to trap | **provided**: `HTRAP` bits 0 to 14 (1.5); `common/pdp64exec.c` `gtrap` (`:370`) |
| 8 | The direct-mapping windows and `HALT` always exit, so L1 cannot reach real physical memory | **provided**: 6.14.14, 6.14.12; `common/pdp64exec.c:666`, `:607`, `:734` |
| 9 | L0 can inject an exception or a virtual interrupt into L1 or L2 | **provided**: the write aliases `GEVA`, `GECAUSE` and the guest bank registers exist for this (1.11); virtual lines (1.8) |
| 10 | L0 can learn that L1 edited a page that L1 uses as a stage-2 table, without write-protecting it | **provided if the invalidate rule is architectural** (see the detail below the table): L0 needs no trace |
| 11 | **Instructions that mean something else at `H` = 1 than at `H` = 0** cannot be trapped: `RTI`, `RTT`, `PUSHX`, `POPX` (they name `HEPC` and `HEPSW` at `H` = 1, `EPC` and `EPSW` at `H` = 0: `hypervisor-design.md` 1.2 rule 3, 3.4); taking an exception (it writes `EPC`, `EPSW`, `EVA`, `ECAUSE` and fetches the *guest's* `VBR`); reading `MMUCTL` (L1 untranslated at virtual `H` = 1 needs the real bit clear, but its world switch reads the guest's value) | **missing**: no `HTRAP` bit covers them (the bits trap *writes* of `PTBR`, `MMUCTL`, `VBR`, not reads or exception entry). This is a **correctness** gap (an unmodified L1 misbehaves, in its own VM only), **not a safety** gap: whatever L1 does at `H` = 0, L0's tables and trap bits still govern it |
| 12 | The memory type of a composed translation (L2 stage 1, L1 stage 2, L0 stage 2) | **missing**: the combining of even two stages is "left to the hypervisor" (`hypervisor-design.md` 1.7, errata 195 decision 12 open sub-point; hole H6 and amendment A3 of `best-of-breed-mmu-virt.md`). L0's shadow must pick one type; without an architectural rule L0 cannot be verified and the rule of errata 65 against 97 is unreconciled (A4) |

**Detail of points 10 and 6.** The shadow is a cache of L1's table, and the architecture already says it may be stale until L1 invalidates: G-d tells the hypervisor to issue `TLBIA` after a change to a stage-2 entry that was valid; an entry that was *invalid* and becomes valid needs nothing, because the shadow simply has no entry and faults. So the x86 solution that Turtles used for its table (read-only pages that trap L1's writes) is **not required** on the pdp64, and L0 does not have to know *where* L1's tables are: it learns that something changed from L1's `TLBIA`, which traps (point 7) and which it answers by discarding the whole shadow of that tag, the same cheap rule as G-c. One condition, which is a **decision for the owner (D4 below)**: the manual must say that a stage-2 entry may be used from the cache until the invalidate, and that a hypervisor *must* invalidate after changing a valid entry. G-d says the second half; the first half is 6.8.4(c) and (d) for stage 1 and has to be extended to stage 2 in words. (The model today discards more than required, errata 196, so it cannot show a stale shadow by itself; the same architectural cache of errata 196 is what would.)

**Point 11 in the form an L1 would hit it.** A world switch by the hypervisor of 2.7 uses `MFSR` of all 12 guest registers *including* `MMUCTL`, and L1 runs untranslated at virtual `H` = 1. L0 can keep the real `MMUCTL` bit 0 clear while L1 runs, but then L1 reads 0 where it expects L2's value. An L1 written to know it is nested can avoid the problem (set the `MMUCTL` trap bit and keep its own copy, never reading), as the hypervisor of `tests/qemu/hv-first.S` happens to: it uses no `RTI`, `RTT`, `PUSHX`, `POPX` and no read of `MMUCTL` at its own level (checked by reading the file, lines 1 to 120; its `RTI` lines 206 to 228 are the *guest kernel's*). It is the same class of problem as Arm's second and third kinds of instruction (2.2), and RISC-V solved it with `VTSR` and `VTVM`.

### 4.3 The one small amendment: a `VH` bit (point 11)

**Proposal (not decided, an amendment of the kind the principle asks for: the smallest change that closes the hole by construction).** `HTRAP` bit 15 becomes `VH`, "the guest is running its own hypervisor's code". While it is set, at `H` = 0, **`RTI`, `RTT`, `PUSHX`, `POPX`, a read of `MMUCTL`, and every exception entry the guest would take, exit** (one new exit code, 22, with the vector of exits and the same syndrome registers). L0 sets the bit exactly while L1 is at virtual `H` = 1 and clears it while L2 runs.

* *By construction:* an L1 that follows the architecture of 1.2 and 2.2 cannot observe that it is not at `H` = 1.
* *Hardware (measured with the repository's method, `vh_trap`, my scratch Verilog):* the decision, one bit and an OR of six instruction-class signals the decoder already makes: **14 NAND2 equivalents and 1 flip-flop** (20 in all). The routing of an exception entry to the exit machinery instead of the guest's entry is an **estimate of 50 to 200**. Together **70 to 220 NAND2 equivalents, 0.02% to 0.1% of the scalar core**.
* *Executor and QEMU:* about 30 to 60 lines in `common/pdp64exec.c` (an estimate from the size of the existing `gtrap` and `gexit` and their call sites: `:370-381`, 68 lines of the file mention the hypervisor state by my grep); the QEMU target needs nothing more because the executor is shared (errata 196).
* *Compatibility:* it is discoverable and compatible by the rule that already exists: a machine without it faults on a write of a one to bit 15, and software probes (`common/pdp64exec.c:471`).
* *Against:* it is not a safety feature (principle P5, no new hardware unless it buys safety) and nobody needs it in version 1, since there are no nested guests. That is why the recommendation of section 8 is to reserve it by a sentence, not to build it.

### 4.4 Gates for point A, summed

**Zero** for the safe nesting of the accepted design (points 1 to 10: every one *provided*, checked in the table above). **Plus 70 to 220 NAND2 equivalents** if the owner wants an *unmodified* L1 to work (`VH`). **Plus zero** for memory-type composition (a four-by-four table, already proposed as A3 whether or not there is nesting). I could not check by running anything that the executor's exits behave as the table says for L1-style sequences (nothing nested has been run); the checks are by reading the code and the design.

## 5. Design point B: hardware-assisted nesting

Two independent pieces, and only the first has precedent.

### 5.1 B1: redirect L1's hypervisor-register moves to a page (Arm NEVE, RISC-V NACL)

**What it is.** A new register `HNREG` (number 148, the first free number of the block): a 28-bit physical page number and an enable bit. When enabled, a guest's `MFSR` or `MTSR` of a hypervisor register is a physical load or store at `HNREG` page + 8 * number instead of an exit (the read-only registers still exit on a write). `GENTER`, `TLBI`, `TLBIA`, stage-2 faults and everything else still exit, and L0 reads or writes the page at those exits. Because the pdp64's hypervisor is untranslated (errata 195 decision 2), the page address is a plain physical address, the simplification NEVE's authors asked for ("a page-aligned physical address ... to avoid the need to perform alignment checks or handle address translation faults").

**State:** 28 + 1 flip-flops per core.

**Gates (measured, my scratch Verilog `nv_redirect` against the accepted design's decision alone `hv_exit_only`, same flow as `tools/gates`):** the decode of "register 128 to 147, redirect or exit" is **25 NAND2 equivalents against 12, so +13**; the address is the page register and the number, **wiring only (0 gates)**; the 29 flip-flops are **174 NAND2 equivalents**. **Estimate, not measured:** the control that turns a register move into a physical load or store through the load and store unit, bypassing translation, **500 to 2,000** (the record's load and store block is 8,000 to 10,000 with its sequencer). **Total about 700 to 2,200, 0.2% to 1.0% of the scalar core.**

**What it buys, counted.** For the pdp64, per exit of L2 that L1 handles (section 4.1 point 5): without B1 each of L1's reads of `HEXITR`, `HCAUSE`, `HEPC`, `HEPSW`, `HEVA`, `HGPA`, `HINSN` and each write of the registers it loads for the next entry is an exit to L0. **Counted from `hypervisor-design.md` 1.3, 2.7 and the hypervisor of `tests/qemu/hv-first.S` (lines 79 to 115), an estimate of the L1 exits per L2 exit:**

| L1's handler | Hypervisor-register moves and `GENTER` that exit to L0 | With B1 |
|---|---|---|
| the minimal hypervisor of `hv-first.S`, a hypercall: `mfsr hexitr`, `mfsr hcause`, `genter` | 3 | 1 (`GENTER`) |
| the same, a stage-2 fault: `mfsr hexitr`, `mfsr hgpa`, `tlbia` (trapped by the `TLBIA` bit), `genter` | 4 | 2 |
| a full world switch to another guest (2.7): reads of 7 exit registers and 4 guest-bank registers, writes of 6 aliases and banks, `HTRAP`, `GTBR`, `VIRQ`, `VIVEC`, `VTOFF`, `VCPUID`, `HEPC`, `HEPSW`, then `GENTER` | about 25 to 30 | about 1 to 3 |

So B1 removes **most of the exit multiplication of this design** (about 3 to 30 exits per L2 exit to 1 to 3), the same reduction that NEVE and NACL were designed for. The cost to L0 is that it must read the page at each exit instead of keeping the values in registers: that is cheap.

### 5.2 B2: a hardware walk through two second stages (no precedent found)

**What it would be.** L2's access: stage 1 (L2's tables) then L1's stage 2 then L0's stage 2 for L1, all by the hardware walker, with no shadow. Registers: a second root register `GTBR1` (29 flip-flops, 174 NAND2 equivalents). Walker: a second level of the recursion that the two-stage walk already has.

**Table reads on a cold miss (computed from the repository's formula, 6.14, nm + n + m).** Translating one L2-guest-physical address through two stages of three levels each takes 3 x 3 + 3 + 3 = 15 reads; the L2 walk of three levels needs that for each of its three table pointers (and one more read of each entry) and once for the final address: 3 x 16 + 15 = **63 reads**, against 15 for two stages and 3 for one. A shadow (point A) keeps the hardware walk at 15 and moves the cost to the first fault of each page.

**Translation buffer.** The entry would be keyed by the L2 tag and by the L1 tag, so that a change of L0's table for L1 could invalidate "every guest of L1" in one operation. **Measured** (my scratch `tlb32_t32` against `tlb32_t16`, 32 entries): 18,402 logic and 3,104 flip-flops against 13,152 and 2,592, so **+5,250 logic and +512 flip-flops, +8,322 NAND2 equivalents per 32-entry buffer**. For the core's two buffers, the record's factor of 1.5: **an estimated +12,500**. It is avoidable: without a second tag L0 invalidates each of L1's guests by its own tag in a loop.

**Walker control.** Estimated **3,000 to 10,000** NAND2 equivalents. *Basis:* the record gives translation as 34,000 to 38,000 for two buffers, the walker, permissions and the windows, of which my synthesis puts a 32-entry buffer at 19,876; the walker's share is therefore of the order of 10,000, and a second recursion level plus the second-root logic adds a fraction of it. This is a guess at the fraction, not a measurement, and I mark it low confidence.

**Total, B2: about 16,000 to 23,000 with the second tag (about 3,000 to 10,000 without it), 5% to 10% of the core (1% to 5% without the tag).** No architecture in the sources I read does this (section 2). It would be a **bold change** in the sense of errata 198 (a departure from all three baselines) and the principle allows that only for a big win. There is no win to measure: a shadow gives the same walk cost, and the exits to fill it are first-touch only.

### 5.3 Verification and proof impact

* **seL4** has no verified hypervisor configuration on RISC-V or x86-64, and verifies the hypervisor configuration of Arm only (`docs/encoding-work/sel4-fit.md` headline 3 and the table row 19, which cite the seL4 documentation; the review's table says "Hypervisor configuration ... verified on ARM only"); I found **nothing about nesting** in that review (a search for the word returned no match). So the pdp64 would neither gain nor lose proof coverage from nesting (the review's own wording for the hypervisor in general), but would put *new* state in the model that someone eventually models.
* **Software nesting adds no hardware state**, so the machine's architectural model that a verifier would write (the walker "has a fixed meaning", `sel4-fit.md` section 1) is unchanged. The proof obligation moves to L0's software: the shadow is correct if it is the composition of the two tables at every point where L1 has invalidated.
* **B1** adds one register and 20 memory-mapped views of registers; the verifier must model "a register move is a store". **B2** adds a three-stage walk and a second tag; that is a larger change to the one component the review calls a fixed meaning. I rank it worse for proof.

## 7. Performance: what exit multiplication would do to the pdp64's exits

Sources are the other architectures' measurements (section 2); **the pdp64 has no measured exit cost**, so the figures below are *numbers of exits*, not time.

| | One exit of L2 becomes, in exits of L1 to L0 | Source |
|---|---|---|
| x86, no shadowed VMCS | 13 (a cheap exit) to 31 (an I/O exit) | Turtles, section 4.1.1 |
| x86, `vmread`/`vmwrite` not trapping | 2 to 14 | same |
| Arm Armv8.3, trap and emulate | 82 to 126 (a hypercall) | NEVE, section 3 |
| pdp64, point A, minimal hypervisor | about 3 to 4 | counted, section 5.1 |
| pdp64, point A, full world switch | about 25 to 30 | counted, section 5.1 |
| pdp64, with B1 | about 1 to 3 | counted, section 5.1 |

Two reasons the pdp64's figures are far lower than Arm's: its hypervisor state is **20 registers, not the hundreds Arm has** (so L1 touches few), and its virtual interrupts and virtual timer need **no** hypervisor register moves to deliver (L0 writes the real `VIRQ`; the hardware delivers). Two reasons they could still hurt: each exit is also L0's *emulation* work (the walker of 4.1 point 3 is far heavier than a register move), and a shadow stage 2 fills one L0 exit per first touch of a page of L2. **Overhead of the nested case as measured elsewhere:** 6% to 14.5% for CPU-bound and mixed workloads on x86 with good paging and I/O (Turtles, 4.1), 14% to 33% on Arm v8.3 for CPU-bound ones and up to a factor of 40 for network-heavy ones without NEVE (NEVE, section 7). These are ranges from different machines and workloads and **do not transfer**; I give them to show the shape (CPU-bound cheap, exit-heavy expensive) and that hardware redirection is the lever.

## 8. Recommendation under the principle (errata 198, R521)

The principle: start from best of breed; amend minimally so the problem closes by construction; a bold change only for a big win.

* **Best of breed for nesting is "software first, assist after measurement"**: x86, Arm and RISC-V all did it in that order (section 2, "The pattern"). The pdp64's accepted design is *already* the software baseline: 6.14.7's exits carry a syndrome rich enough to emulate (points 1 to 9 of 4.2), and the stage-2 and tag rules make a shadow stage 2 possible without tracing L1's pages (point 10).
* **Do not build nesting now.** The cost is 45 to 105 person-days for a feature no first software needs, with no proof coverage anywhere in the sources I read, and the version-1 rule costs nothing (the real hypervisor's policy is "inject an illegal-instruction exception", `hypervisor-design.md` 6.6). The owner's own position, "ok with not allowing nested guests", agrees.
* **Keep the door open by three sentences, at no gate and almost no work:**
  1. say in 6.14.7 that the exit of a guest's hypervisor register or `GENTER` is **the** mechanism, and that a hypervisor may emulate it (it already is; the manual says "traps" only);
  2. reserve `HTRAP` bit 15 for `VH` and exit code 22 for it (the bit is already reserved and probing is already defined, so the cost is one sentence);
  3. settle the memory-type composition (A3) and the stale-cache guarantee (D4 below): needed by the base design whether or not there is nesting.
* **Do not build B2.** It departs from all three baselines, costs 5% to 10% of the core and 30 to 60 person-days, and buys nothing a shadow does not. **B1 only when a measured L1 exit count asks** (it is a new register in the free block with an enable that is off by default, so it can be added later without changing any existing program: the same way Arm and RISC-V added theirs).
* **If the owner later wants an unmodified L1 to work**, `VH` is the one amendment: 70 to 220 NAND2 equivalents, 30 to 60 lines of executor. That is the cheapest item in this document and the only one that cannot be done in software after the silicon exists, which is why it is worth a reservation now.

## 9. Decisions for the owner, each with a recommendation

*Each can be answered **accept / change / not decided**. Nothing is recorded as decided until you answer.*

1. **Keep 6.14.7, "no nested virtualization in version 1", and build no nesting now?** *Recommendation:* accept. Point A costs about 45 to 105 person-days and no safe-nesting gates; the version-1 rule costs none.
2. **State in 6.14.7 that a guest's use of a hypervisor register or `GENTER` is an exit that carries enough information for the hypervisor to emulate it, and that a hypervisor *may* do so (it is what the model already does)?** *Recommendation:* accept. A sentence; it makes the policy visible as a policy.
3. **Reserve `HTRAP` bit 15 as `VH` ("the guest runs its own hypervisor's code": `RTI`, `RTT`, `PUSHX`, `POPX`, a read of `MMUCTL` and every exception entry exit) and exit code 22 for it, without building it?** *Recommendation:* accept the reservation. *If you want it built* (measured about 20 NAND2 equivalents, estimated 70 to 220 in all, 30 to 60 lines of executor), say "change".
4. **State in the manual that a stage-2 entry may be used from the cache until an invalidate, and that a change to a valid entry must be followed by the invalidate (rule G-d), so that a nested hypervisor needs no trace of the pages it uses as stage-2 tables?** *Recommendation:* accept. It is already G-d; this is the other half in words.
5. **Settle the memory type across stages by amendment A3 of `best-of-breed-mmu-virt.md` (the more restrictive of the types, a four-by-four table), whose absence also blocks a verifiable shadow?** *Recommendation:* accept; it is that document's decision 5 and nesting does not change its answer.
6. **Is an unmodified guest hypervisor a goal at all (that is, will `VH` ever be built), or only a hypervisor written to know it is nested (the hypervisor of `hv-first.S` is one)?** *Recommendation:* not decided; revisit when a first nested use exists. A nested-aware L1 works without `VH`.
7. **Build the register page `HNREG` (B1) later, when a measurement of L1's exits per L2 exit asks for it, as a new register in the free block with the enable off at reset?** *Recommendation:* accept "later"; about 700 to 2,200 NAND2 equivalents and 15 to 30 person-days then.
8. **Build a hardware walk through two second stages (B2)?** *Recommendation:* do not; it departs from all three baselines, costs an estimated 16,000 to 23,000 NAND2 equivalents (5% to 10% of the core) and 30 to 60 person-days, and a shadow stage 2 gives the same walk cost.
9. **Count the accepted design's own guest tag in the gate record (measured here: +8,828 NAND2 equivalents for a 32-entry buffer, an estimated +13,000 for the core's two buffers) and re-run the estimate of `vec-gates.md` for translation when the owner next asks for it?** *Recommendation:* accept; it was missing.
